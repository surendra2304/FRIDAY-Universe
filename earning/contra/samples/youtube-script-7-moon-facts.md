## SCRIPT

### HOOK (0:00–0:30)

The Moon is moving away from you right now. Not in some poetic sense — literally. Every single year, it drifts about four centimetres further from Earth. And that is the least weird thing about it.

In the next eight minutes, you'll find out why astronaut footprints could outlast the pyramids by a hundred million years, why the Moon smells like gunpowder, and the one thing about the Moon that scientists still can't fully explain. Stick around for fact number five — it's the one that changes how you look at the night sky.

### INTRO (0:30–0:50)

We've all stared at the Moon. It's the one thing in the sky every human in history has seen. But almost everything you think you know about it is either incomplete or flat-out wrong. These are seven facts that sound made up — and every one of them is real.

### FACT 1 — The Moon is leaving us

The Moon is drifting away from Earth at about 3.8 centimetres a year — roughly the speed your fingernails grow. We know this precisely, because the Apollo astronauts left laser reflectors on the surface, and we've been bouncing lasers off them ever since.

Here's why that matters: one day, the Moon will be too far away for a total solar eclipse. A total eclipse only works because of a cosmic coincidence — the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it's also about 400 times further away, so the two look exactly the same size from Earth. As the Moon keeps drifting, that perfect alignment breaks. In roughly 600 million years, there will never be another total solar eclipse. We're living in the only era that gets them.

### FACT 2 — Footprints that outlive civilisations

The Moon has no air, no wind, and no rain. So there is nothing to erase a footprint. The boot prints left by the twelve Apollo moonwalkers could still be there in a hundred million years — the only thing slowly wearing them down is tiny micrometeorites.

Think about that. The pyramids are about four and a half thousand years old, and we treat them as ancient beyond imagining. Those footprints will outlast them twenty thousand times over.

But the dust those boots kicked up hides a nasty surprise — and it's the reason NASA treats Moon dust like a hazard. More on that in a minute.

### FACT 3 — The Moon smells like gunpowder

When the Apollo astronauts climbed back into the lander and took their helmets off, they could smell the Moon dust they'd tracked in. Every one of them described it the same way: like spent gunpowder. Like a firework that just went off.

And here's the part I promised you: the smell is the least dangerous thing about that dust. Lunar dust is made of tiny, jagged grains, sharp as broken glass, and it carries an electric charge, so it sticks to everything. Breathe it in and it irritates the lungs. NASA lists it as one of the top health risks for future Moon missions. The dust doesn't just smell strange — it's genuinely hazardous, and solving it is one of the real engineering problems of going back.

### FACT 4 — The Moon has earthquakes

They're called moonquakes, and the Apollo missions left seismometers on the surface that recorded thousands of them. The deep ones are triggered by the gravitational pull of the Earth itself, squeezing and stretching the Moon as it orbits.

And they behave nothing like earthquakes. On Earth, a quake that size would be over in a minute or so. On the Moon, the shaking can rumble on for ten minutes or more — because the Moon is dry and rigid, it rings like a bell instead of absorbing the energy.

### FACT 5 — The Moon has two faces, and nobody fully knows why

This is the one from the hook. Look at the Moon tonight: the side facing us is covered in huge dark plains, the maria — ancient lava flows. Now look at photos of the far side, the half we never see from Earth. Almost none of those dark plains. It's nearly all cratered highlands. Same Moon, two completely different faces.

And here is the part scientists still argue about: nobody fully agrees why. The best ideas involve an ancient collision, or uneven heating in the Moon's early history — but after fifty years of data, it's still an open question. The most familiar object in the night sky, and we still can't explain why it looks the way it does.

And while we're on the subject of things the Moon does to Earth — it's quietly stealing something from us. Time itself. I'll prove it at the end.

### FACT 6 — There is water on the Moon

For decades, everyone assumed the Moon was bone dry. It isn't. In the permanently shadowed craters near the poles — places sunlight has never reached — temperatures drop below minus 150 degrees, and water ice sits trapped there, untouched for billions of years.

We know because in 2009, NASA deliberately crashed a probe into one of those craters and measured the plume it kicked up. Water, confirmed. And it matters, because future astronauts won't need to bring all their water with them. They could drink it, split it into oxygen to breathe, and even turn it into rocket fuel. The Moon isn't just a rock to visit — it's a gas station on the way to Mars.

### FACT 7 — The Moon is stealing our time

Remember the promise. Here it is. The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, and that drag is slowly braking our planet's spin. Every century, the day gets longer by about 1.7 milliseconds. It sounds like nothing — until you run the clock backwards.

Four hundred million years ago, a day was about twenty-two hours long. We know that from fossil corals, which laid down daily growth rings like tree rings, and counted roughly four hundred days in a year. The Moon has been stretching our days for billions of years, a fraction of a second at a time — and it's still doing it right now.

### BONUS BEAT

Quick bonus, because it deserves a mention. Only twelve people have ever walked on the Moon — and the last one to do it, Gene Cernan, stepped off the surface in December 1972. More than fifty years ago. Every person alive who has stood on another world did it before most of us were born. The next boot prints on the Moon are still waiting to be made — and when they are, they'll last a hundred million years too.

### OUTRO

So: the Moon is leaving us, it smells like gunpowder, it shakes, it hides water in permanent darkness, and it's been quietly making our days longer since before there were trees. Next time you look up, remember — you're looking at the weirdest neighbour we've got.
