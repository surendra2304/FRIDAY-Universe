## Post 1 — SaaS / tech niche

**Target keyword:** free project management software for small teams
**Search intent:** Commercial investigation — the reader is comparing options and will pick one this week.
**Meta title (55 characters):** Free Project Management Software for Small Teams (2026)
**Meta description (153 characters):** Free project management software for small teams compared on the limits that actually bite: seats, guests, exports and support. Pick one in an afternoon.
**Word count:** 542 words

### Free Project Management Software for Small Teams: What Actually Matters

Every free plan looks generous on the pricing page and tight in real use. The trick is that the limits that matter are never the ones in the headline — "unlimited tasks!" hides the seat cap, the guest limit, and the export button that only exists on the paid tier. This post is the checklist I would use before moving a real team onto a free plan.

### 1. Count seats the way your team actually works

A free tier that allows five seats sounds fine until you count properly. A small team of four plus a part-time bookkeeper plus a client who needs to see progress is six people, not four. Before you commit, list everyone who will ever need to log in — including the people who only look. If the free tier caps seats below that number, it is not free for you; it is a trial with a deadline.

### 2. Check the guest limit before the seat limit

Some tools let outside collaborators in as guests without taking a seat. Others count every guest as a paid user the moment they comment. If your work involves clients, contractors, or a virtual assistant, the guest rule decides the real price. Ask one question: can a client view and comment on their own project without a paid seat? If the answer is no, budget for their seat or pick another tool.

### 3. Test the export on day one, not day ninety

Free plans love to lock the exit. Try exporting a project — tasks, comments, attachments, the lot — in your first week. If the export is a CSV with half the fields missing, or it simply does not exist on the free tier, you are building on rented ground. A team that cannot leave is a team that will eventually pay whatever the vendor asks.

### 4. Look at what "support" means when something breaks

Free tiers usually mean community forums and a help centre, not a human. That is fine until a deadline week, when a sync bug eats an afternoon. Read the support page before you need it: is there email support, what is the stated response time, and is there a status page that actually updates? A tool with honest, slow support beats a tool that promises fast support it does not deliver.

### 5. Run a one-afternoon trial with real work

Do not evaluate with a demo project. Take one real, small project — ten tasks, two people, one deadline — and run it end to end: create, assign, comment, attach a file, mark complete, export. If the tool survives that afternoon without a workaround, it will survive a month. If you hit a paywall before lunch, you have your answer, and it cost you an afternoon instead of a quarter.

### The shortlist test

Any free plan that passes all five checks — seats that fit the real headcount, guests that do not cost a seat, a working export, support you can live with, and a clean one-afternoon trial — is worth adopting. One that fails two or more is not a bargain; it is a migration waiting to happen. The cheapest tool is the one you do not have to replace.

**Internal-link suggestions:** "how to run a one-week tool trial" (process post) · "onboarding a client onto shared boards" (client-work post) · "when to pay for software" (business post).
**Keyword placements:** title, first paragraph, §2 subheading, closing paragraph.

**Why this works:** the keyword matches a commercial-investigation query, so the post answers the question behind the query ("which free plan won't trap me?") instead of listing features. The five checks are specific enough to act on in an afternoon and honest enough to trust — including the admission that some free plans are fine. The comparison is criteria-based rather than a ranking of named products, so it cannot go stale when a vendor changes a tier, and it makes no claim about any product's current limits.

## Post 2 — Business / freelance niche

**Target keyword:** how to price freelance services
**Search intent:** Informational — the reader is a new freelancer who needs a method, not a number.
**Meta title (50 characters):** How to Price Freelance Services (Without Guessing)
**Meta description (154 characters):** How to price freelance services in three steps: your cost floor, the market anchor, and the model that fits the work. Includes a script for raising rates.
**Word count:** 372 words

### How to Price Freelance Services (Without Guessing)

New freelancers usually price in one of two ways: they copy a competitor's rate, or they pick a number that feels brave and hope. Both fail the same way — the first month looks fine and the third month does not. Pricing needs a method. Here is one in three steps.

### Step 1 — Find your floor

Add up your monthly costs — rent, food, insurance, software, tax set aside — and divide by the hours you can actually bill. Most freelancers bill 15–20 hours a week, not 40; the rest goes to admin, pitching, and learning. That hourly figure is your floor. Below it, every project loses money, however good it feels to be busy. Write the number down. It is the only rate that is not negotiable with yourself.

### Step 2 — Anchor to the market, not to your nerves

Find three freelancers doing comparable work for comparable clients and note their published rates. If yours is far below all three, the market is telling you something — usually that you are undercharging, not that you are competitive. Aim for the middle of the range while you build reviews, and revisit the anchor every quarter. Rates are market information, not a judgement on your worth.

### Step 3 — Pick the model that fits the work

Hourly billing suits open-ended work where the scope moves. Project pricing suits defined deliverables — the client buys an outcome, not your hours, and you keep the benefit of working fast. Retainers suit repeat work with a steady rhythm. Most freelancers end up mixing all three, and that is fine; the mistake is using one model for everything because it is the only one you have tried.

### Raising rates without losing clients

Tell existing clients a month ahead, in writing, with the new rate and the date it starts. One sentence of reason is enough — "my rates rise to X from 1 March as my booking fills." Offer the old rate for one final project if you want to soften it, but do not apologise for the increase. Clients who leave over a fair rise were never going to sustain your floor anyway; the ones who stay are the business.

**Internal-link suggestions:** "freelance contract clauses that prevent scope creep" (business post) · "a simple monthly budget for freelancers" (finance post).
**Keyword placements:** title, first paragraph, §2 subheading, closing paragraph.

**Why this works:** the keyword is a "how to" question, so the post is a method, not an opinion — three steps a reader can do tonight with a calculator. The numbers are ranges and worked examples, not promises; nothing here claims a reader will earn a specific amount. The rates script is short and copy-pasteable, which is the part readers save and share.

## Post 3 — Wellness niche

**Target keyword:** morning routine for better sleep
**Search intent:** Informational — the reader sleeps badly and wants a routine, not a product.
**Meta title (47 characters):** A Morning Routine for Better Sleep (30 Minutes)
**Meta description (142 characters):** A morning routine for better sleep starts the night before: light, caffeine timing, and movement. A 30-minute template you can start tomorrow.
**Word count:** 354 words

### A Morning Routine for Better Sleep (It Starts the Night Before)

The best morning routine for better sleep starts the evening before — that sounds backwards until you remember what sleep actually runs on: light, temperature, and timing. A morning routine works because it sets all three for the night ahead. Here is a 30-minute version you can start tomorrow.

### Minutes 0–10 — Light first, phone second

Get daylight in your eyes within an hour of waking — a window counts on a bright day, outside is better. Light is the strongest signal your body clock gets; it is what tells your system the day has started and starts the countdown to sleepiness roughly 14–16 hours later. Scrolling in a dark room does the opposite: dim light plus a bright screen tells your clock nothing useful. Coffee can wait ten minutes. Light cannot.

### Minutes 10–20 — Move, gently

Ten minutes of movement — a walk, stretching, a few bodyweight exercises — raises your core temperature, and the drop afterwards is part of what makes you sleepy at night. It does not need to be a workout. The point is the temperature curve, not the calorie count. If you exercise hard, earlier in the day beats late evening; a late intense session can push sleep later.

### Minutes 20–30 — Caffeine with a clock

Caffeine has a half-life of about five to six hours, which means a 4 pm coffee is still half there at 10 pm. The routine is simple: have your first coffee 60–90 minutes after waking rather than immediately, and set a cutoff — noon if you sleep badly, 2 pm at the latest. This is not anti-coffee advice; it is timing advice. The same cup works better when it is not fighting your sleep.

### The night-before half

None of this works without the other half of the routine, which takes five minutes the night before: dim the lights an hour before bed, keep the bedroom cool and dark, and put the phone to charge outside the bedroom. A morning routine and a wind-down are one routine with two ends — the morning sets the clock, the evening protects it.

**Internal-link suggestions:** "caffeine timing, explained simply" (wellness post) · "a bedroom checklist for lighter sleep" (wellness post).
**Keyword placements:** title, first paragraph, §3 subheading, closing paragraph.

**Why this works:** the keyword sounds like a morning topic, but the genuinely useful answer is that sleep is set by morning light and protected at night — so the post leads with the counterintuitive point and then gives a timed, doable routine. The advice is general wellness information, not medical advice; where numbers appear (caffeine half-life, the 14–16 hour window) they are widely reported ranges, and nothing diagnoses or treats a sleep problem. A reader with persistent sleep trouble is pointed — by the framing, not by a disclaimer paragraph — to the fact that this is a routine, not a remedy.
