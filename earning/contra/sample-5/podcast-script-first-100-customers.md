## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)

Here's a number that should worry every new business owner — and it's not a sales figure. It's zero. Zero people searching for your business by name, because nobody has heard of you yet.

Most small businesses don't fail because the product is bad. They fail because nobody ever finds them. You can have the best bakery, the best bookkeeping service, the best handmade soap on your street — and if the first hundred customers never hear about you, the business quietly starves.

Today's episode is the fix for that. Three things you can do this week, with no ad budget, to get found, get trusted, and turn one customer into the next ten. Let's get into it.

*[MUSIC STING — 3 seconds]*

### INTRO / HOST WELCOME (0:50–2:10)

Welcome back to the show. I'm Surendra, and this is the podcast for people building small businesses with more skill than marketing budget. If that's you — if you're great at the work and terrible at being discovered — you're in the right place.

Quick framing before we start. "First 100 customers" is not a vanity number. It's the point where a business stops depending on the owner's friends and family and starts running on its own reputation. Before a hundred, every sale feels like a favour. After a hundred, you have reviews, referrals, and repeat buyers — the business starts selling itself a little. So the question is: how do you get there without spending money you don't have on ads?

Three segments today. One: be findable where people already look. Two: turn one customer into the next. Three: show up where your customers already gather. And at the end, I'll answer a listener question about whether you should be on every social platform at once. Spoiler: no. Definitely not.

Let's start with being findable.

### SEGMENT 1 — Be findable where people already look (2:10–4:50)

When someone in your town needs what you sell, what do they actually do? They don't open a directory. They don't scroll a newspaper. They type something into Google, or they ask in a local group chat. So the first job is to be there when that search happens — and the good news is the single most useful tool for it is free.

It's your Google Business Profile. If you have a physical location, or you serve customers in a defined area, claim it. It's free, it takes about twenty minutes, and it's the reason one bakery shows up on the map and the bakery next door doesn't. Fill in everything: real opening hours, real photos of the actual place and the actual work — not stock photos — your phone number, and a short description that says what you do in plain words a customer would use. Not "artisanal solutions." Say "custom birthday cakes, delivered in [your town]." Say what people search for.

Then the part most owners skip: reviews. When you get a happy customer, ask them — right then, while they're happy — to leave a Google review, and make it easy by sending them the direct link. You can generate that link from your profile dashboard. And when reviews come in, answer every single one. Every one. Thank the good ones by name, and answer the bad ones calmly and publicly, because your reply isn't really for the unhappy customer — it's for the next hundred people reading it, deciding whether they can trust you. A business with twelve reviews and a thoughtful reply to each one looks more trustworthy than a business with two hundred reviews and silence.

One more piece of being findable: say the same thing everywhere. Your business name, address and phone number should match — exactly — on Google, on Instagram, on your website, on any directory you're listed in. Search engines get confused by mismatches, and confused search engines don't rank you. It takes an hour to check, once, and it quietly helps for years.

So segment one, in one sentence: be on the map, be reviewed, be consistent. It costs nothing but an afternoon.

### SEGMENT 2 — Turn one customer into the next (4:50–7:35)

Here's the uncomfortable truth about referrals: happy customers almost never refer you on their own. Not because they don't like you — because it doesn't occur to them. Nobody finishes a great haircut and thinks, "I must go and tell three people about this." They think about their day. Your job is to make the referral easy to think of — and easy to do.

The best moment to ask is the moment of delight. The cake is delivered and the customer is smiling. The website is live and the client just saw it. The repair is done and the thing works again. That's when you say one sentence, and it has to be specific. Not "tell your friends about me." That's too vague to act on. Say: "If you know one person who's planning a birthday this month, I'd love an introduction — here's my card, and I'll take good care of them." One person. One situation. Something the customer can picture.

And give them something to forward. A short message they can copy into a chat. A photo of the work with your number on it. A simple offer for the friend, not a discount for the customer — "your friend's first order includes free delivery" travels further than "get ten percent off your next visit," because it makes your customer look generous instead of self-interested.

Then — and this is the part that separates businesses that grow from businesses that stall — follow up with the people who already bought from you. Not with a sales blast. With something useful. A bakery messages last month's customers: "We're testing a new sourdough on Saturday, first ten loaves are half price for regulars." A bookkeeper emails clients in January: "Here's the one document to pull together before tax season." You're not chasing them. You're reminding them you exist, at the exact moment they might need you again — or know someone who does.

One customer, asked well and followed up with honestly, is worth more than a hundred strangers seeing an ad. That's the whole engine of the first hundred: each customer is a bridge to the next one, and you build the bridge by asking — specifically, at the right moment, and making it easy.

### SEGMENT 3 — Show up where your customers already gather (7:35–10:05)

Segment three is about going where attention already exists, instead of trying to buy it or build it from scratch.

Start with the businesses around you that serve the same customer but don't compete with you. The wedding photographer and the florist. The gym and the meal-prep kitchen. The accountant and the business lawyer. Pick one, and propose something small and concrete: a joint offer, a shared stall at a local market, a simple cross-recommendation — "my clients get ten percent off your first session, yours get the same from me." One partnership that actually happens beats ten networking events where you collected business cards and never called anyone.

Then, the community version of the same idea. Your customers are already gathered somewhere: the school gate, the weekend market, the local Facebook group, the running club, the coworking space. Don't show up there to sell. Show up there to be useful. The baker who brings free samples to the school fair is remembered. The plumber who answers a "who do you recommend?" thread with genuinely helpful advice — not a pitch — gets recommended the next time the question comes up. Be the person who helps first, and the recommendations follow.

And if you make content at all, make it answer the question your customer is already asking. Not "ten reasons we're great." Answer the thing they type into Google at 11pm: "how much does a custom cake cost," "what should I ask a bookkeeper before hiring one," "how do I fix a leaking tap without calling anyone." One honest, useful answer a week, in whatever format you can sustain — a short video, a post, a voice note — builds trust faster than any slogan, and it keeps working for you while you sleep.

That is the playbook. Be findable. Turn one customer into the next. Show up where your people already are. None of it needs an ad budget. All of it needs consistency — a little, every week, beats a lot, once.

### LISTENER QUESTION (10:05–11:15)

Time for the listener question. This one came in from Priya, who runs a home bakery and asks: "Should I be on every social platform at once — Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, YouTube — so I don't miss anyone?"

Short answer: no. Longer answer: be excellent on the one platform where your customers actually are, and ignore the rest until the first one is working without you thinking about it. For a bakery, that's almost certainly Instagram — it's visual, it's local, people browse it when they're hungry. Post three times a week, same days, same quality, answer every comment, and show the work: the mess, the oven, the finished cake, the happy customer. One platform done properly will bring you more customers than four platforms done badly, because four platforms done badly just means four places where you look inactive.

Pick one. Do it properly. Add a second only when the first one feels easy. That's the rule.
