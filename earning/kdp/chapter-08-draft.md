# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 8 (Draft)

## Chapter 8 — The First Offer: Selling to a Small List Honestly

You have a list now. Not a big list — around 100 people, or close to it — but
a real one. These are people who chose your lead magnet, opened your welcome
emails, and in some cases replied to you. They know your name.

This chapter is about making your first offer to that list: one small thing
to buy, offered once, in plain language, to people it genuinely fits. No
launch event. No countdown timer. Just an honest email that says: here is the
next step, here is what it costs, and here is how to take it — or not.

If selling makes you uncomfortable, good. That discomfort is what keeps you
from turning into the kind of seller you would not want to buy from.

### Why 100 right people out-buy 10,000 strangers

A large audience looks impressive and sells poorly. A small list of the right
people looks unimpressive and sells well. Buying is a trust decision, and
trust is built in the specific, not in the general.

Think back to Day 1. Your list was built around one person and one problem.
For the physiotherapist in private practice, the problem was that Instagram
took hours every week and brought in no patients. For the first-time Etsy
seller, the problem was listing products nobody found. Your lead magnet
solved one small piece of that problem. Your five welcome emails — deliver
the magnet and tell your story, teach something useful, teach again with
proof the idea works in real life, invite them to take a next step, and ask
them a question — showed those readers who you are and whether your advice
actually helps.

Ten thousand strangers have had none of that. They do not know whether you
understand their problem, whether your advice works, or whether you will
still be there next week. Your 100 subscribers do. Some have replied to your
emails. Some have told you, in their own words, what they are stuck on. That
is not a small audience. That is a warm room.

A small list also lets you sell something small and specific. You do not need
a course or a membership. You need one offer that is the obvious next step
after your lead magnet — for the same person, with the same problem. When the fit is that tight, you are reminding people you have already helped, not persuading strangers.

One honest expectation first. Most of your 100 subscribers will not buy your
first offer. That is normal, not a failure. Some are not ready. Some do not
have the money right now. Your first offer is a test of whether one specific
offer fits one specific group of people. If a handful buy, you have learned
something real. If nobody buys, you have also learned something real — the
last section of this chapter tells you how to read it.

### What a first offer is — and what it is not

A first offer is small, specific, and finishable. It solves the next problem
after the one your lead magnet solved, for the same person. It has one price,
one way to buy, and a clear description of what the buyer gets.

It is not a big course, a monthly membership, three pricing tiers, or a
"founding member" deal with a deadline that does not exist. All of those add
decisions, and decisions are where small offers go to die.

Two examples, using the avatars from Chapter 1. Each is the next step after
the free help, not a different topic:

- **For the physiotherapist** whose lead magnet was a checklist for turning
  one patient question into a week's worth of posts: a 45-minute, one-to-one
  review of their profile and last month of posts, ending with a written
  list of the five changes most likely to bring enquiries. One call, one
  written plan, one price.
- **For the first-time Etsy seller** whose lead magnet was a listing-title
  template: a written review of five listings — titles, photos, descriptions
  and tags — with exact rewritten titles they can paste in. Delivered by
  email within a few days, one price.

If you sell a service, the offer is usually your time in a small package — one call, one review, one audit. If you sell a product, a small digital item that goes deeper than the free one. Keep it small enough to deliver well, and to explain in one paragraph.

Before you write the offer email, answer four questions in writing. If you
cannot answer one in a sentence, the offer is not ready: who is it for (your
Day-1 avatar, not "everyone on my list")? What problem does it solve that the
lead magnet did not finish solving? What exactly does the buyer get, and by
when? What does it cost, and how do they pay?

### Pricing a first small product or service

There is no formula in this book that hands you the "correct" price, because
there is not one. Your price depends on your time, your costs, what your
avatar can realistically pay, and what comparable help costs where they live.

Two honest ways to arrive at a first price — pick the one that fits, and
write down your reasoning so you can revisit it instead of guessing again.

**Price your time.** If the offer is an hour of your time plus preparation
and a written follow-up, work out what that hour is worth to you and add the
preparation time. If the total feels too high for your avatar, the offer is
too big — shrink the offer, not your hourly value. A 30-minute review costs
less than a 90-minute review and is easier to buy.

**Price it as a first version.** Your first offer is a test, and a lower
price makes the test easier to run: more people can say yes, and you learn
faster whether the offer fits. That is not undervaluing yourself; it is
paying for information with a smaller margin on the first few sales. Be
clear with yourself that the low price is a launch decision, not a permanent
one — and never tell buyers a price is "discounted" from a higher price that
never existed.

Whichever method you choose: one price, not three tiers. Say what is included
and what is not — "a 45-minute call and a written plan sent within three
days" is clear; "ongoing support" is not, and it will cost you evenings. Say
how payment works before they click, including whether payment is upfront and
what happens if they need to reschedule. Have a simple refund or reschedule
policy and state it — for a service, reschedule once for free up to a day
before; for a digital product, fix it or refund it if the file does not open
or is not as described. Write the policy before you need it.

And remember: the free emails do not stop because someone did not buy.

### The soft-offer email structure

Your first offer goes out as one email to your list. Not a three-email
launch sequence, not a sales page with a video — one email, in the same voice
as your welcome sequence. Six parts, each doing one job. Keep it short enough
to read on a phone in a couple of minutes.

**1. Remind them who you are and what you have already given them.** One or
two sentences: "Over the last few weeks I have been sending you one practical
idea each week for turning your clinic's posts into patient enquiries."

**2. Name the problem the offer solves, in their words.** Use the language
your subscribers used in their replies and in your Day-1 conversations. If
nobody told you that, do not invent it — name the problem your lead magnet
left unfinished, and say so plainly.

**3. Describe the offer in one paragraph.** What it is, who it is for, and
what the buyer walks away with. "A 45-minute review of your profile and your
last month of posts, on a call, ending with a written list of your five most
useful changes." If a reader cannot repeat the offer back to a friend in one
sentence, rewrite it.

**4. Say what is included, what is not, and what it costs.** This is the
paragraph where vagueness costs you sales. Put the price in the email itself.


**5. Say how to buy, in one step.** One link or one reply. "Reply to this
email with 'review' and I will send you the booking link" is a perfectly
good first-offer mechanism — it also starts a conversation, which is worth
more than a click at this stage. If you use a payment link, test it yourself
first, as a buyer would.

**6. Close without pressure.** Give the reader a dignified way not to buy:
"If this is not for you right now, nothing changes — the weekly email
continues as normal." Then invite a reply even from non-buyers: "If you are
stuck on something, tell me what it is. It helps me choose what to write
about next." Replies are the most valuable thing a small list produces.

Send the offer once. If you want a reminder, send it once, a few days later,
and say plainly that it is a reminder and the last one. At 100 subscribers, every person who feels
chased is a person who may leave — and you worked seven days to earn them.

### The ethics chapter: selling without lying

This is the part of the book that will cost you money in the short term and
save your list in the long term. Most of what passes for online selling
advice is a set of tricks for making people buy faster than they can think.
You are not going to use them. Not because they never work — they often do,
once — but because your list is small, your reputation is the whole business,
and a reader who feels manipulated does not come back.

**No fake scarcity, no fake urgency.** Do not say "only three spots left"
when there are ten, or "price goes up on Friday" when it does not. Do not run
a countdown to a deadline you invented, and do not re-offer a "one-time" deal
the following week. If you genuinely have limited capacity — you can only do
four one-to-one reviews a week — say that, truthfully, and say what happens
when they are full: a waiting list, or the next available week. Real limits
are believable. Invented ones are detectable.

**No rented or invented testimonials.** Only use words from real buyers or
real readers, with their permission, and only in context. A testimonial from
someone who has never bought from you, borrowed from another product, or
written by a friend who has never used the offer is a lie. If you have no
testimonials yet, say so by saying nothing: your first offer does not need a
testimonial section. When you do ask a buyer for a few words, ask for honest
words, not praise — "What changed, if anything, after you used it?" — and
use exactly what they said.

**No income claims you cannot prove.** Do not promise buyers a particular
amount of money, a particular number of patients or sales, or a particular
result. The result depends on their situation, their effort, and factors
outside your control. Say what the offer *is* and what it *does*; let the
buyer decide what it might be worth. Do not present your own results as
typical.

**No bought lists, no spam.** Your offer goes to your own list — people who
subscribed to hear from you. Not to a list you bought, scraped, or borrowed,
and not to people who unsubscribed. If someone unsubscribes after your offer
email, let them go without a follow-up.

Before you send, read the email once as a buyer: is every sentence true, and would I feel respected receiving this? If not, fix it first.

### What to do when nobody buys

You send the offer. You wait a day. Nobody buys. The silence feels like a
verdict on you, your list, and the whole seven days. It is not a verdict. It
is data — but only if you read it properly. Do not slash the price in a
panic, and do not send the same offer louder to the same people. Both teach
you nothing and cost you trust. Diagnose instead, changing one thing at a
time.

**Did people see it?** Check whether the email was delivered and opened — as
a basic check, not a score. If very few opened it, the problem may be the
subject line or the send time, not the offer.

**Did people reply?** Replies matter more than opens. If people replied —
even to say "not for me right now" — the offer was seen and considered, and
the replies are the diagnosis. If nobody replied, re-read your closing: did
you actually invite a reply, or only a purchase?

**Was the offer the right next step?** An Etsy seller who downloaded a
listing-title template needs a listing review, not a course on product
photography. A mismatch here is the most common reason a good list ignores a
good offer. Ask three subscribers directly — by reply, not by survey — what
the useful next step after the lead magnet would be.

**Was the offer clear?** Ask a friend outside your niche to read the email
and repeat the offer back in one sentence: what they would get, what it
costs, how to buy. If they cannot, the offer was unclear, and clarity is the
cheapest fix there is.

**Was the price the problem — or the timing?** Price is the easiest thing to
blame and often the wrong diagnosis. Check the questions above first. If the
email was seen, understood, and matched to the right problem, and still
nobody bought, then price — or timing — may genuinely be the issue. If so,
do not slash the price in public. Shrink the offer instead: a shorter review,
a smaller package that solves a smaller piece of the problem. That tests
whether price was the barrier, without teaching your list to wait for
discounts. And remember that you sent this offer in Week 3, once the welcome
sequence had finished for your first subscribers. Some readers have only just
met you. Keep sending the weekly email from Chapter 7, keep answering
replies, and mention the offer again in a few weeks, once, in a normal
email — not a relaunch, just a mention.

If you still do not know, ask your list directly: "I offered [the offer] last week and nobody took it up — was it the price, the timing, or just not the right thing?"

Nobody buying your first offer does not mean nobody will ever buy anything
from you, your list is worthless, or you should start over with a new niche.
It means one offer, at one price, at one moment, did not fit. Change one
thing, try again, and keep the list warm.

### Your finished asset: the soft-offer email template

Copy this, fill in the blanks, and send it as one email. Keep your own voice;
the brackets are the only parts you must change.

---

**Subject:** [A next step, if you want one] — for example: "A closer look at
your listings, if you'd like one"

Hi [first name / "there"],

Over the last few weeks I have been sending you [what your weekly email is —
for example: one practical idea each Tuesday for turning your clinic's posts
into patient enquiries].

Several of you have told me [the problem in their words — for example: that
the hard part is not knowing what to post, it is knowing whether any of it is
working].

If that is you, I have a small next step. I am offering [the offer in one
sentence — for example: a 45-minute review of your profile and your last
month of posts, on a call].

Here is what you get:

- [Included item 1 — for example: the 45-minute call, at a time you choose]
- [Included item 2 — for example: a written list of your five most useful
  changes, sent within three days]
- [Included item 3 — for example: one follow-up email if you have questions]

What you do not get: [for example: ongoing posting, or done-for-you content
— say it plainly].

The price is [price, in your currency]. You can [how to buy — for example:
reply to this email with "review" and I will send you the booking link / use
this link: ___].

[One honest limit, only if it is real — for example: I can do four of these a
week, so if this week is full I will offer you the next available week.]

If this is not for you right now, nothing changes — the weekly email continues
as normal. And if you are stuck on something, reply and tell me what it is.
It helps me choose what to write about next.

[Your name]

---

Fill it in, read it once as a buyer, fix anything that is not true, and send
it. That is the whole launch.
