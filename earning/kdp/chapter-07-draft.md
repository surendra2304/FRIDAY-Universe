# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 7 (Draft)

## Chapter 7 — Day 7: From 50 to 100 — and What to Send Every Week

By the end of today you will have two things: a plan that gets you from fifty subscribers to a hundred, and a weekly rhythm you can keep for the next month without burning out. That is the whole job of Day 7. You are not launching anything new today. You are looking honestly at what Day 6 taught you, doing more of what worked, and setting up the one habit that decides whether this list lives or dies: sending one good email, every week, on the same day.

Read that last sentence again, because it is the most important one in this chapter. The list you built this week does not survive on the launch. It survives on the second email, and the fifth, and the twelfth. Most beginner lists do not die from a bad signup page. They die from silence — the owner sends a welcome sequence, then nothing for six weeks, then an apologetic "sorry I've been quiet" that nobody asked for. Your subscribers joined for a rhythm you promised them in your one-sentence promise from Day 1: one practical idea every Tuesday, one search-and-listing fix every Friday. Today you make that rhythm real.

First, a word about the number. A hundred subscribers is the goal of this book, and you should aim for it. But a hundred is not a magic line where everything changes. A list of sixty people who reply is worth more than a list of a hundred people who ignore you, and a list of a hundred people who ignore you is worth less than a list of fifty who talk back. Everything in this chapter is measured against that standard: does it bring you people who reply, or just people who add to a count?

### Step 1: Double what's already working

Open your Day-6 tally. Four sources — warm circle, swaps, signature, communities — and a mark beside each subscriber. Add them up. One of them will have brought the most subscribers. That is your winner, and your first job today is to do more of it before you try anything new.

This sounds obvious, and it is the step most people skip, because doing more of what worked feels less exciting than finding a new tactic. Resist that. A channel that brought you twenty subscribers in a day is a channel with more to give. A channel that brought you two is not going to bring you twenty tomorrow because you try it harder.

Here is what doubling looks like for each source:

- **If the warm circle won:** you have more names. Go back to your contacts with fresh eyes and find the second twenty — the people you didn't think of yesterday, the friends of the friends who signed up, the past clients you forgot. Send the same personal message from your Day-6 launch kit, one at a time. And ask your new subscribers a favour: the welcome sequence you built on Day 4 ends by asking a question — when someone replies to it, thank them, and ask whether they know one person the list might help. A subscriber who recommends you to a friend is doing your best marketing for you, and it costs them nothing.
- **If swaps won:** send three more pitches today, to the next three peers on your list, and write back to the partner whose swap worked best to thank them and ask about a second mention next month. One good swap partner who mentions you twice is worth more than five one-off mentions.
- **If your signature and links won:** strangers are finding you, which means your profile and your older posts are doing the work. Add the link to the two or three places you missed yesterday, and look at which page or post brought the subscribers. If one old post keeps sending people, write a follow-up to it.
- **If communities won:** stay in those communities and keep answering questions, the way Chapter 6 described — helpfully, without links, following the rules. Do not increase the volume of mentions. Increase the volume of helping. The subscribers will follow at their own pace.

And if nothing clearly won — if your subscribers came in ones and twos from everywhere — that is information too. It means no single channel is carrying you yet, and your best move is a second round of the warm circle, because that is the one channel you fully control. Send the second twenty messages. Then, next week, look again.

One thing not to do today: do not start a new channel. Do not open an account on a platform you have never used, do not start a video series, do not launch a second lead magnet. Every new channel is a week of learning before it brings a single subscriber. You have a channel that works. Feed it first.

### Step 2: The weekly newsletter rhythm — one email, three sources

Here is the fear every beginner has about a weekly email: "What will I write about every week?" The answer is that you will never again have to invent a topic from nothing, because a weekly email has only three sources, and you already have all three in your life.

**Source one: something you learned.** You are a few honest steps ahead of your reader — that was the Day-1 standard, and it is still the standard. Every week you learn something in your own work: a fix that worked, a mistake you made, a tool you tried, a question a client asked that you had to think about. Write that down, in plain words, as one short email. The physiotherapist who tried a new way of asking patients for a review and got three bookings from it has next Tuesday's email. The Etsy seller who changed one word in a listing title and started appearing in search has next Friday's email. You do not need to be an expert. You need to be a careful reporter of your own week.

**Source two: something you answered.** Every question a subscriber asks you — by reply, in a community, in a conversation — is an email waiting to be written. When one person asks a question, several of your subscribers are wondering the same thing and haven't asked. Answer it once, well, for the whole list. This is the most reliable source of the three, because the topic is chosen by your readers, not by you. Keep a running list of questions people ask you. When the list reaches three, you have three weeks of emails.

**Source three: something you made.** Your lead magnet, a checklist, a template, a short guide, a before-and-after example from your own work. Once a month, share something you made and explain how to use it. Not every week — a list that only ever promotes its own products gets dull fast — but regularly enough that your subscribers see you building things for them, not just talking.

One email a week uses one source, not all three. Pick the best thing from the week — the most useful, the most asked-about, the most honest — and write about that. The other two wait for next week. A short email about one useful thing beats a long email about three things every time, because a short email gets read to the end, and an email read to the end gets a reply.

The shape of the email is the same every week, so you never have to design it again: one line that says what the email is about, in plain words, not a clever teaser ("The one change that got my listing found in search"); the useful thing in the middle — three hundred to six hundred words, one idea, explained with an example from your own work, steps not theory; one question at the end that your reader can answer in one sentence, the same habit as the final welcome email; and a short sign-off in your voice — who you are, and when the next email arrives. No apology for emailing, and no long footer of links. One link if you need it, to the thing you mentioned. Nothing more.

Send it on the same day every week, the day you promised on your signup page. Tuesday means Tuesday. If Tuesday is a bad day for you, change the promise on the page and then keep the new day. The day matters less than the regularity. Regularity is what turns a stranger into a reader who expects you — and a reader who expects you is a reader who replies.

A note on length and frequency, because this is where beginners quietly quit: if one email a week feels like too much, you are writing emails that are too long. Shorten them. A useful email of three hundred words, sent every week, beats a brilliant email of two thousand words sent once a month and then abandoned. And if you miss a week — you will, one day — do not send an apology email about missing a week. Just send the next useful email on the next scheduled day. Your readers did not hire you to be perfect. They signed up to learn something useful, regularly.

### Step 3: Subject-line testing for tiny lists

Your email platform will show you open rates, and you will be tempted to obsess over them. At your size, don't — or rather, obsess over the right thing, in the right way.

A subject-line test is simple: you send the same email to your list with two different subject lines and see which one more people open. At the size of a big list, that tells you something. At the size of a list of fifty or a hundred, it tells you almost nothing, because the numbers are too small for a real comparison. If twenty-five people get subject line A and twenty-five get subject line B, and twelve open A while nine open B, you have learned nothing reliable — three people is not a pattern, it is a Tuesday. The honest thing to do with a tiny list is to test slowly, and to read the results like a person, not like a statistician.

Here is how to test subject lines when your list is small:

- **Test one thing at a time, and keep a notebook.** Write down each week's subject line with the number of opens and replies beside it. After a month, read the list as a whole: you are looking for a pattern across four or five emails, not a winner in one. Do the plain, specific subject lines get more opens than the clever ones? Do questions beat statements? You will see it in the list, if you keep the list.
- **Compare subject lines to each other, not to a number in your head.** There is no "good" open rate for a list of sixty people, and anyone who tells you one is selling you something. What matters is whether this week's subject line did better or worse than last week's, with the same list, in the same rhythm. Your list is its own baseline.
- **Treat replies as the real test.** An email that gets ten opens and four replies has done its job better than an email that gets thirty opens and no replies. A subject line that brings people who read and answer is worth more than one that brings people who open and delete. When you look at your notebook each month, circle the emails that got replies, and look at what their subject lines had in common.
- **Do not test more than one change a week.** If you change the subject line, the send day and the email length all at once, you will never know which change mattered. Change one thing, keep everything else the same, and write down what you changed.

And one rule that matters more than any testing method: never write a subject line that promises something the email does not deliver. "The mistake costing you patients" is fine if the email names a real mistake. It is a lie if the email is a general chat about marketing. A misleading subject line might win an open this week and lose a subscriber next week, and at your size you cannot afford to lose subscribers you worked a full day to win. Plain, specific and true — the same standard as every other sentence in this book.

### Step 4: Cleaning the list without fear

Sooner or later — probably within the first month — someone will unsubscribe. Your stomach will drop. You will wonder what you did wrong. Here is the truth, plainly: **an unsubscribe is not a failure. It is the list working as designed.**

Your list is for one person with one problem. Everyone who is not that person should leave, and the sooner the better. A subscriber who never opens your emails costs you nothing in money on a free tier, but costs you something worse: they teach you nothing, they never reply, and they make your list look less engaged than it is. A clean list of eighty people who read you is better than a list of a hundred where twenty never open anything. You are not collecting names. You are building a conversation.

Cleaning the list means three small habits:

1. **Let unsubscribes happen, and do not chase them.** Every email you send has an unsubscribe link — your platform puts it there, and the law in most places requires it. Never remove it, never hide it, never email someone after they unsubscribe to ask why. Thank them silently by making the next email better.
2. **Once a month, look at who has never opened anything.** Your platform will show you this. If someone has received your last four or five emails and opened none of them, send them one short email — not a guilt trip, a genuine question: "Are these emails still useful to you? If not, no hard feelings — you can unsubscribe below, and I'll wish you well." Some will reply and tell you what they actually need, which is gold. Some will unsubscribe, which is fine. Some will do nothing, and after another month you can remove them yourself, quietly. Removing someone who never reads you is not rude. It is honest housekeeping.
3. **Remove bounced addresses when your platform flags them.** An address that bounces — the mailbox is full, closed, or typed wrong — will keep bouncing, and repeatedly emailing a dead address can hurt your deliverability, which is the Day-5 lesson about landing in inboxes rather than spam. Most platforms remove hard bounces for you. Check yours does, and if it asks you, say yes.

Do not clean the list out of anxiety, and do not clean it every day. Once a month, ten minutes, three habits. A list you are afraid to clean is a list you are treating as a trophy. It is not a trophy. It is a room full of people you promised something to, and the kindest thing you can do for the people in the room is to let the people who want to leave, leave.

### Step 5: The metrics that matter at 100 subscribers

When you reach a hundred subscribers, your platform will offer you a dashboard full of numbers. Most of them are noise at your size. Here are the only ones that matter, in order:

1. **Replies.** How many people wrote back to your last email? This is the number this whole book is about: a reply means someone read, thought, and trusted you enough to answer. One reply per email is a healthy list at a hundred subscribers. Zero replies for a month means the problem has drifted, or the question at the end of your emails is not one anyone can answer in one sentence — go back to Day 1 and check the problem against real conversations.
2. **Subscriber growth, week by week.** Not the total — the direction. Are you gaining two or three a week, or losing them? Write the number down every Monday, in the same notebook as your subject lines. A list that grows slowly and steadily is healthy. A list that grows in bursts and then shrinks is telling you the launch brought the wrong people, or the weekly emails are not keeping the promise the signup page made.
3. **Unsubscribes per email.** One or two per email is normal. A sudden jump — five or six from one email — is worth reading carefully: what did that email promise, and did it deliver? A jump after a specific email is the most honest feedback you will ever get, and it is free.
4. **Opens, read loosely.** Opens tell you whether your subject lines and your send time are roughly right. They do not tell you whether your emails are good — a person can open an email and delete it in three seconds. Use opens to compare your emails with each other, as Step 3 describes. Never use them to judge your worth as a writer.

What not to measure: how your numbers compare with someone else's list, or with a number you read online. Every list is different — different people, different problems, different promises — and comparing yours with a stranger's is how a good list starts feeling like a failure. Your baseline is your own list, last week. Beat last week, gently, and you are doing it right.

### What the next month looks like

Day 7 ends the seven days, but the work does not end here. The next month is three habits: one email a week, on the same day — one source, one useful thing, one question, with the numbers kept in your notebook; one growth action a week — a second-round message, a new swap pitch, a guest post, a helpful community answer — rotated, never all in one week and none the next; and one review at the end of the month, where you open the notebook, see which source and which emails worked, and write three sentences about what you learned. Those three sentences choose next month's growth action and email topics.

That is the whole system. It is not glamorous, and it will not make you famous. It will give you a hundred people who know your name, trust your advice, and answer when you write — and that, as Chapter 8 will show, is enough to sell something honest to, when you are ready.

### Your Day-7 finished asset: the weekly rhythm checklist and email template

Copy this into a document you will actually open every week. Fill in the brackets once, then reuse it.

**A. The weekly email template (one email, one source, one question)**

> **Subject line:** [The useful thing, stated plainly — e.g. "The one change that got my listing found in search"]
>
> [Opening line: what this email is about, in one sentence.]
>
> [The useful thing: 300–600 words. One idea, with an example from your own work or your reader's world. Steps, not theory.]
>
> [One question your reader can answer in one sentence — e.g. "What is the one listing you wish someone would find?"]
>
> [Sign-off, two lines: who you are, and when the next email arrives.]

**B. The weekly rhythm checklist (do this every week, on your sending day)**

- [ ] Pick this week's source: something I learned / something I was asked / something I made
- [ ] Write the email using the template above (300–600 words, one idea, one question at the end)
- [ ] Read it aloud once; cut anything that does not help your one person
- [ ] Send it on the promised day, at roughly the same time as last week
- [ ] Answer every reply within a day — thank them, and ask a follow-up question where it fits
- [ ] Write the numbers in the notebook: subject line, opens, replies, new subscribers, unsubscribes
- [ ] Do this week's one growth action (second-round message, swap pitch, guest post, or helpful community answer)

**C. The monthly review checklist (do this once a month, ten minutes)**

- [ ] Read the notebook: which growth source brought the most subscribers?
- [ ] Which emails got replies? What did their subject lines have in common?
- [ ] Which email lost the most subscribers, and what did it promise?
- [ ] Remove or re-engage subscribers who have opened nothing in the last four or five emails
- [ ] Write three sentences about what you learned, and choose next month's growth action from them
- [ ] Write down your subscriber count and the date — this is your new baseline
