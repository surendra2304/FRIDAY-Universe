# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 1 (Full Draft)

## Chapter 1 — Day 1: Pick the One Person and the One Problem

By the end of today you will have written two sentences. That sounds like a small
day's work, and it is — deliberately. Every failed email list I've ever seen failed
on Day 1, not on Day 30. They picked an audience of "everyone", a topic of
"everything I know", and a promise of "great content". Nobody subscribes to great
content. People subscribe because a specific problem in their life is about to get
smaller.

Today is about making your list specific enough that a real person, reading your
signup page on their phone, thinks: *this is for me.*

### Why "everyone" is nobody

Imagine two signup pages.

The first says: *"Join my newsletter for tips on business, life and productivity."*

The second says: *"Every Tuesday, one practical idea for physiotherapists in
private practice who want their clinic's Instagram to bring in patients — without
dancing on camera."*

The first page is for everyone, so it converts no one. The second page will lose
99% of the internet — and convert the remaining 1% at a rate the first page will
never touch. Your first 100 subscribers are not hiding in the 99%. They are the
1% who feel *named*.

This is the whole secret of Day 1, and honestly of the entire book: **a small list
of the right people is a business asset; a big list of the wrong people is a
chore.** A coach with 100 subscribers who reply can fill a workshop. A creator
with 10,000 followers who scroll past cannot fill a comment thread.

### Step 1: Describe one person, not a market

Forget demographics for a moment. Markets don't open emails; people do, usually
while half-asleep, usually deleting everything that isn't obviously for them.

Take a notebook or open a blank document and answer these questions about ONE
specific person — ideally a real person you know, have served, or have been:

1. What do they do all day? (job, business, or role)
2. What are they trying to achieve this year that actually matters to them?
3. What have they already tried that didn't work?
4. What do they complain about — in their own words, not marketing words?
5. When in their week does the problem hurt most?
6. What would a small, real win look like for them in the next 30 days?

Write in sentences, not bullet-point labels. "A 34-year-old marketing manager" is
a label. "Priya runs social media for a small hotel group, her boss judges her on
screenshots of follower counts, and she has no idea which of her posts actually
produced a booking" — that's a person you can write an email to.

If you are the person — if you're building the list you wish had existed when you
started — even better. Your past self is the most honest avatar there is, because
you know exactly which advice was useless.

### Step 2: Have five conversations (or reconstruct them)

The biggest Day-1 mistake is inventing a problem at your desk. Problems are found
in conversations. Before you write your list's promise, talk to five people who
match your avatar. In person, on a call, in messages — the medium doesn't matter.
Ask exactly these three questions:

1. "What's the hardest part of [the thing] right now?"
2. "What have you tried so far?"
3. "If a magic email arrived every week that actually helped, what would it do?"

Then be quiet and write down their *words*. Not your interpretation — their words.
If three of the five say some version of "I start motivated and quit by week two",
you have not found a motivation problem; you have found a *sticking-with-it*
problem, and your list should be about that.

Can't reach five people today? Reconstruct the conversations from evidence:
comments on videos in your niche, questions in forums and groups, reviews of books
and courses your avatar buys (the 3-star reviews are the honest ones), and your
own messages from when you had the problem. It is slower and less reliable than
talking — so if you use the reconstruction route, mark your problem statement as a
*draft* and revisit it after your first ten subscribers, when real replies start
correcting you.

### Step 3: Narrow to one problem you can actually shrink

Your avatar has twenty problems. Your list gets one — the one that is:

- **Painful now**, not someday (people trade emails for relief, not for trivia),
- **Specific** enough to describe in one sentence,
- **Shrinkable by email** — you can genuinely help in 300–600 word instalments,
- **Yours to talk about** — you've done it, studied it, or are visibly doing it
  now and reporting honestly. (You do not need to be the world's expert. You need
  to be a few honest steps ahead, or an unusually careful researcher. What you
  must never do is invent results you don't have. Chapter 8 returns to this.)

Write the problem in your avatar's words: *"I post on Instagram three times a week
and I still can't tell if any of it brings bookings."* That sentence is worth more
than a month of generic content ideas, because every future email, lead magnet and
signup headline will be built from it.

### Step 4: Write the one-sentence promise

Your list's promise combines the person, the problem and the rhythm:

> **For [one person] who [one problem], this list sends [what], [how often], so
> they can [small real win].**

Examples, using the avatars from this chapter:

- *For physiotherapists in private practice whose Instagram doesn't bring
  patients, this list sends one practical clinic-marketing idea every Tuesday, so
  they can turn posts into bookings.*
- *For first-time Etsy sellers who list products nobody finds, this list sends
  one search-and-listing fix every Friday, so they can get their first ten sales.*

Read yours aloud. If it sounds like it could be any list, it isn't finished. If a
friend in your avatar's situation would say "wait, that's me" — it is.

5. The five people I talked to (or the evidence I used): ____________________

### What not to do today

Don't buy a domain, don't pick an email platform, don't design a logo, and don't
write your lead magnet. All of that is Day 2 onwards, and all of it gets easier —
and cheaper — once these two sentences exist. Lists fail from vagueness, not from
ugly logos.
