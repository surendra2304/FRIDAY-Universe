# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 2 (Draft)

## Chapter 2 — Day 2: Build a Lead Magnet People Actually Want

By the end of today you will have one finished thing a stranger would trade an
email address for. Not a plan for one. One small, finished file — a checklist, a
template, a short guide, or a short email course — that solves one small piece
of your one person's one problem.

Yesterday you wrote two sentences: your one person, and your one-sentence
promise. Today those sentences do their first real job. If you skipped Day 1,
go back and do it now. A lead magnet built without an avatar and a promise is a
freebie for nobody in particular, and nobody in particular never subscribes.

Today's work is an afternoon, not a week. Pick one small slice of the problem,
pick the simplest format that fits it, build it with free tools you already
know, give it a title that names the result, and test it against one question:
*would a stranger trade an email address for this?*

### What a lead magnet is — and what it isn't

A lead magnet is a small, specific, useful thing you give away in exchange for
an email address. The smallness matters more than the usefulness. People do not
hand over their email address for something they might read someday. They hand
it over for something that fixes a small, annoying problem today, in one
sitting. Your reader asks one question, in about three seconds: *is this worth
the emails?* Your lead magnet is your answer.

A lead magnet is not your newsletter. "Subscribe for weekly tips" is not a lead
magnet; it is a request for trust you have not earned yet. A lead magnet is not
your life's work, either. A 60-page ebook nobody finishes is worth less than a
one-page checklist somebody actually uses on Tuesday morning. Boring and
finished beats ambitious and half-done, every single time.

### The "one problem, one sitting" rule

Every lead magnet in this book follows one rule: **one problem, one sitting.**

One problem means the lead magnet solves one small piece of the Day-1 problem —
not the whole problem. Your whole problem is the reason your list exists; your
lead magnet is the first step into it. If your one person is a physiotherapist
whose Instagram doesn't bring patients, your lead magnet is not "The Complete
Guide to Clinic Marketing." It is one piece: the captions, the bio, the first
three posts. One piece, solved completely.

One sitting means your reader can use it and finish it in a single sitting — an
evening, a lunch break, the time it takes to drink a coffee. If they have to
come back to it three times, they won't. If they finish it and feel the small
win, they trust you, and they remember you. That trust is what the welcome
sequence in Chapter 4 builds on.

Test every idea before you build it. Say it out loud: *"After one sitting with
this, my reader will have [one concrete thing done]."* If you can't finish that
sentence, the idea is too big. Cut it in half. Then cut it in half again.

Take the two avatars from Chapter 1. The physiotherapist's problem: *"I post on
Instagram three times a week and I still can't tell if any of it brings
bookings."* One sitting-sized piece: write seven captions that each end with a
booking prompt. After one sitting, she has a week's worth of posts written.

The first-time Etsy seller's problem: *"I list products nobody finds."* One
sitting-sized piece: rewrite one listing title and its first two lines so a
buyer searching for the product can actually find it. After one sitting, she has
one listing fixed — and she knows how to fix the rest.

Both readers end the sitting with something *done*, not something *learned*.
Done beats learned. People trade email addresses for done.

### The four formats that work for beginners

There are dozens of lead-magnet formats in the world. Beginners need four. Each
one below comes with what it is, when to pick it, an example for one of our two
avatars, and the trap that ruins it. Pick the format that fits your slice — not
the format you wish you were good at.

#### 1. The checklist

**What it is:** a one- or two-page list of steps, in order, with boxes to tick.
The reader prints it or keeps it open on their phone and works through it.

**Pick it when:** your slice is a process — steps that get missed, done out of
order, or forgotten under pressure. Checklists are the easiest format to build
well and the hardest to get wrong, so they are the default choice if you are
unsure.

**Example:** "The 10-Point Post Checklist: Before You Publish Any Clinic Post"
— does the caption name one patient problem, say what to do next, link to
booking, use a real clinic photo? Ten points, one page.

**The trap:** a checklist that is really a lecture. Every item must be an action
the reader can do and tick off. "Write captions that connect" is advice — it
cannot be ticked. "Caption names one patient problem in the first line" is an
action — it can.

#### 2. The template

**What it is:** a fill-in-the-blanks document your reader copies and completes
— a script, a layout, a tracker, a set of sentence starters. The hard part, the
structure, is already done. The reader supplies only their own details.

**Pick it when:** your slice is something your reader has to produce from a
blank page and dreads starting. Captions, emails, listings, budgets, plans,
scripts — anything where the blank page is the problem.

**Example:** "The Listing Title Template: Fill in the Blanks for a Title Buyers
Can Find." A title structure with blanks for what it is, who it is for, and the
detail buyers search for — plus one worked example filled in, so the seller can
see how it looks done.

**The trap:** blanks so vague they don't help, or so specific they only fit
your business. Fill the template in yourself for two different real cases. If
the second one doesn't fit, the blanks are wrong. And always include one
filled-in example: a blank template without an example is a worksheet; with
one, it's a shortcut.

#### 3. The mini guide

**What it is:** a short written guide — five to ten pages at most — that teaches
one small thing properly, with an example and a clear end. Not a book. Not a
course. A guide that respects the reader's evening.

**Pick it when:** your slice needs explaining, not just listing or filling in.
Your reader needs to understand *why* something works before they can do it, or
they keep doing the wrong version of it. A guide earns its length by preventing
a mistake, not by showing off how much you know.

**Example:** "The 7-Day Instagram Fix for Clinics: What to Post When Nobody
Books." Seven short sections, one per day, each with one post idea and one
sentence explaining why that kind of post brings bookings.

**The trap:** scope creep. A mini guide wants to become a book. Every time you
add a section, ask: does my reader need this to get the one win, or does it
just prove I know things? If it is the second, cut it. Past ten pages, you are
writing two guides.

#### 4. The email course

**What it is:** a short sequence of emails — three to seven, one per day — that
teaches the slice step by step. The reader signs up and the first lesson arrives
straight away. This is the only format here that is delivered over time instead
of all at once.

**Pick it when:** your slice has natural steps that work better one at a time,
or your reader's real problem is sticking with something (remember the Day-1
interview answer: "I start motivated and quit by week two"). An email course
turns a single download into a week of small contact with you — a head start on
the welcome sequence in Chapter 4.

**Example:** "Five Days to a Findable Shop." Day 1: one title. Day 2: the first
photo. Day 3: tags. Each email does one thing, and by day five the shop is
measurably easier to find.

**The trap:** writing a course when a checklist would do. Each day's lesson
must be useful on its own. If lesson one is useless without lesson five, you
have a guide cut into pieces, not a course. And don't promise seven days and
deliver three — the number in the title is a promise.

**Build time:** a checklist or template takes an afternoon; a mini guide takes
an afternoon to a day; an email course takes an afternoon to write if each
email stays short (the automatic sending is a Chapter 5 job).

### How to choose: a five-minute decision

If you are torn between formats, answer three questions in order:

1. **What will my reader have done after one sitting?** "Followed steps" —
   checklist. "Filled something in" — template. "Understood something" — mini
   guide. "Done one small thing a day for a few days" — email course.
2. **Which one can I finish today?** Be honest. A finished checklist beats an
   unfinished mini guide. Your list launches tomorrow on Chapter 3's signup
   page; a lead magnet that isn't finished can't be offered.
3. **Which one would my one person actually use?** A busy clinic owner uses a
   checklist between patients. A new seller who hates writing needs a template,
   not a guide. If two formats tie, pick the simpler one — you can build a
   second lead magnet later.

### Cutting the slice: from the big problem to the small piece

Take your Day-1 problem sentence and ask: what is the *first* small piece a
beginner needs, the one that unlocks the rest?

A good slice passes three tests. It is the beginning, not the middle — the
reader can't do the later steps without it. It has a visible finish line — seven
captions written, one listing fixed, twelve boxes ticked. And it leaves them
wanting the next step from you: the slice solves one thing and makes the next
thing obvious, and that next thing is what your weekly emails will teach. Your
lead magnet is the first chapter of a relationship, not the whole book.

A bad slice is the opposite: a middle step that assumes knowledge they don't
have, or a complete solution that leaves them with no reason to stay
subscribed.

Write your slice down now, in one sentence: *"This lead magnet helps [one
person] to [one done thing] in [one sitting]."* If that sentence is clear, the
build will be clear. If it isn't, keep cutting.

### Title formulas: name the result, not the topic

Nobody trades an email address for a topic. They trade it for a result. Write
the title last — after you know exactly what the reader will have done — and
make it name that result. Five formulas that work for beginners:

**1. The number + the result.** *"7 Captions That End in a Booking."* Numbers
promise a finish line. Use the real number, and keep it small: under fifteen
for a checklist, under ten for a guide.

**2. The time + the result.** *"A Findable Listing in One Evening."* Only use
this if the time is honest. Time it yourself while you build — if it takes you
an hour and you promise twenty minutes, your reader will feel lied to, and
they will be right.

**3. The "without" formula.** *"Clinic Posts That Bring Bookings — Without
Dancing on Camera."* The "without" names the thing your reader dreads or can't
afford. Use their words from the Day-1 conversations, not yours.

**4. The mistake formula.** *"5 Listing Mistakes That Keep New Shops
Invisible."* Mistakes work because your reader is already worried they are
making one. Keep the tone helpful, not scolding — show the mistake and the fix,
side by side.

**5. The plain descriptive title.** *"The Clinic Instagram Checklist."* No
cleverness, no promises beyond what it is. A clear plain title beats a clever
confusing one.

Check any title against three questions: does it name the reader? Does it name
the result? Could it belong to any list — and if so, it belongs to no one. Read
it aloud as if you were the reader on their phone; if it takes two readings to
understand, shorten it.

### Building it in an afternoon with free tools

You do not need new software. You need a document, an export to PDF, and
enough care that a stranger takes it seriously. Use the tools you already know:
a word processor or a free online document tool for writing, and a free design
tool only if you want a cover page or a tidier layout. The words matter more
than the layout.

Three hours, with breaks, is a realistic budget. **Hour 1 — outline and draft:**
write the structure first (headings, steps, blanks, lessons), then fill it in,
in plain language, as if explaining it to your one person across a table. Don't
design anything in this hour; designing before drafting is how afternoons
disappear. **Hour 2 — finish, example, and read aloud:** complete the content,
add your worked example, then read the whole thing aloud. Reading aloud catches
the sentences that look fine and sound confusing, and the steps in the wrong
order. **Hour 3 — tidy, export, and test:** one readable font, generous
spacing, headings that stand out. Export as a PDF and open it on your phone —
that is how most readers will first see it. If it is hard to read on a phone,
it is not finished.

Put your name and your list's name on it — a line at the top or bottom is
enough. A lead magnet shared without your name on it is free advertising for
nobody. Save the original editable file, not just the PDF — you will update
this, and it should be editable in minutes. And check the current free tier
before you rely on any tool's free plan. Free tiers change, and this book will
not list limits that may be out of date. If a tool asks you to pay for the one
feature you need, pick a different tool or a simpler format rather than paying
for a list that doesn't exist yet.

### The quality bar: would a stranger trade an email for it?

This is the only quality test that matters. You are not a stranger to your own
work — you know the effort in it; a stranger knows nothing and owes you
nothing. Ask yourself five questions, honestly, before you call it done:

1. **Is the result visible in the title?** Can a stranger tell, in three
   seconds, what they will have done after using it?
2. **Is it for one person?** Would your one person recognise themselves, and
   would someone outside that group correctly feel it isn't for them?
3. **Is it complete?** Does it solve the small piece fully, or stop halfway and
   promise the rest later? A lead magnet that teases is a disappointment.
4. **Is it usable without you?** Could a stranger follow it alone and get the
   result? If it needs you in the room, it isn't finished.
5. **Would you trade your own email for it?** Not your spare address — your
   real one. If you wouldn't, don't ask a stranger to.

Then do the real test: give it to one person who matches your avatar. Don't
explain it. Don't apologise for it. Just ask: *"Would you give your email
address for this? What would you change?"* If they hesitate, ask what is
missing. If they say yes immediately, ask what nearly stopped them. Their
hesitation is your editing list. If you can't find that person today, show the
title to an outsider and ask what they think they'd get — if their answer
matches what you built, your title is honest.

A lead magnet does not need to be perfect; it needs to be useful and finished.
You will make a better one in six months, when you know your readers. What you
must not do is keep polishing this one until the week is gone. Your list
launches tomorrow. Ship the useful version.

### What not to do today

- **Don't build two lead magnets.** One finished one is the day's work.
- **Don't write a book.** If your mini guide passes ten pages, cut it in half.
- **Don't buy anything.** No paid templates, tools, or stock photos. Your list
  has no subscribers yet; spend nothing on it.
- **Don't collect emails you haven't earned.** No bought lists, ever — they are
  other people's addresses, they will mark you as spam, and they poison the
  list before it starts.
- **Don't promise what the lead magnet doesn't deliver.** The title is a
  promise, and Chapter 3's signup page will repeat it.

### The Day-2 production checklist (your finished asset)

Work through this before you close the day. Tick each box only when it is
genuinely done. This checklist is today's finished asset — keep it with the
lead magnet file, and reuse it every time you build a new one.

**The slice**
- [ ] My Day-1 problem sentence is at the top of my working notes.
- [ ] My slice is one sentence: "This helps [one person] to [one done thing]
      in [one sitting]."
- [ ] The slice is the beginning of the problem, not the middle, and it has a
      visible finish line.

**The format**
- [ ] I have picked ONE format: checklist / template / mini guide / email
      course (circle one).
- [ ] I can say what the reader will have done after one sitting, in one
      sentence.
- [ ] Template: at least one filled-in example. Checklist: every item is an
      action that can be ticked.

**The title**
- [ ] The title names the result, not just the topic.
- [ ] The title would make my one person say "that's me" — and an outsider
      would know it isn't for them.
- [ ] Every number and time in the title is true of the file as built.

**The build**
- [ ] The file is finished: written, read aloud once, fixed where it sounded
      confusing.
- [ ] It is exported as a PDF, opens cleanly on a phone, and has my name and
      list name on it.
- [ ] The original editable file is saved, not just the PDF.

**The stranger test**
- [ ] It passes the five quality questions — result visible, for one person,
      complete, usable alone, worth my own real email address.
- [ ] One real person matching my avatar has seen it (or one outsider has read
      the title and described what they expect — and it matches).
- [ ] Nothing in it is bought, copied, or promised that I cannot deliver.
