# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 3 (Draft)

## Chapter 3 — Day 3: The Signup Page That Converts

By the end of today you will have one page that does one job: turn a stranger
who wants your lead magnet into a subscriber. Not a website. Not a sales page.
One page, with one headline, three bullets, one field, and one button.

Yesterday you built the thing. Today you build the door to it.

If you are tempted to skip this chapter because you already have a website, don't. A signup page is not a website with a form bolted on — it is a page with a single purpose, and most signup pages fail because they try to do three jobs at once: introduce you, explain your business, and collect an email. This page does one job, and it does it plainly.

Today's work: write the copy first, put it on a free page, then check it on your phone the way a stranger would. At the end of the day you will have a link you can send to a real person; Chapter 5 connects it to your email platform and welcome sequence.

### Why one page, and why so little on it

Your reader arrives with almost no attention to give you. They clicked a link
from a post, a bio, a message from a friend, or a search. They do not know you.
They are deciding, right now, whether to leave or stay — and they will decide
in seconds, on a phone screen, while doing something else.

Everything you add to the page is something they have to read before they can decide. A long introduction, your life story, a menu — each one is a small reason to leave. The pages that convert best for beginners are the clearest: what they get, who it is for, and what they have to do, in one glance. If an element doesn't help a stranger decide, it doesn't go on the page.

### Anatomy of a one-page signup form

Your page has six parts, in this order — and write them in this order, too.

**1. The headline — one line, the result, for one person.** The headline is the
title of your lead magnet, rewritten as a promise to the reader. It names the person and the result, in their words where you have them — it is the only line most visitors will read fully.

Good headlines, built from the Day-1 promise and the Day-2 titles:

- *For physiotherapists whose Instagram doesn't bring patients: 7 captions
  that end in a booking — write a week's posts in one sitting.*
- *For first-time Etsy sellers whose listings nobody finds: fix one listing
  tonight so buyers can actually find it.*

Bad headlines: *"Join my newsletter!"* names no person, no problem, no result; *"Free tips, tricks and inspiration!"* could be anyone's page.

Write three, then pick the one your one person would recognise as theirs — the Chapter 1 test still applies: would a friend in your avatar's situation say "wait, that's me"?

**2. The subheadline — one or two lines, the how and the how-long.** This
answers the two questions a cautious stranger asks next: *what exactly am I
getting, and how much of my time will it cost?*

- *"A one-page checklist. Work through it in one sitting and your next ten
  clinic posts will each end with a clear next step for the reader."*
"*

The subheadline does not repeat the headline, does not introduce you, and does not say "free" five times — once is enough.

**3. Three bullets — the three things they will have done.** Three bullets, no
more. Each one is a small, concrete outcome — something done, not something
learned. If your lead magnet is a checklist, the bullets are the three most
useful checks. If it is a template, the three things they will have filled in.

For the physiotherapist's caption checklist:

- *Seven ready-to-fill caption templates — a week's posts, written in one
  sitting.*
- *The booking prompt that turns a reader into an enquiry, without sounding
  pushy.*
- *A before-and-after example, so you can see a weak caption become a working
  one.*

For the Etsy seller's template: a title structure buyers can find, one listing fixed tonight, a short checklist for the rest.*

Three rules: start with the outcome, keep it to one line, and never write a bullet that could belong to any lead magnet.

**4. One field — the email address, and nothing else.** Not name and email, not a survey. Every extra field is a reason to leave, and at this stage you do not need the extra information — you need the email address and the permission that comes with it. (You will learn names from replies; Chapter 4's final email asks a question for exactly this reason.) If the tool asks for a first name by default, remove it.

**5. One button — one action, in plain words.** Not "Submit". Not "Sign up".
Those words describe the form, not the reward. The button completes the
sentence your reader is thinking: *I want the [lead magnet].* Good labels: *"Send me the checklist"*, *"Send me the first lesson"*. Name the lead magnet, not the list.

**6. The privacy promise — two sentences, under the button.** A stranger is handing you the address where their bank, their family and their work all write to them. Two honest sentences say what you will and won't do with it — the wording is below; write it before you publish, and mean it.

That is the whole page. If your draft looks too bare, that is usually a sign it is right — bare is what a stranger can read in seconds.

### Writing the copy from your Day-1 promise and Day-2 lead magnet

You already wrote most of this page. Your headline comes from your Day-2 title and your Day-1 promise: the promise says who it is for and what the list sends; the title says what the lead magnet delivers. The headline is those two things in one line.

Your bullets come from your lead magnet's contents. Open the file you finished yesterday and pick the three most useful outcomes in it — if you can't pick three, fix the lead magnet before you dress it up with copy. 

Do the writing on paper or in a plain document before you touch any page tool — page tools invite fiddling, and fiddling is not writing. When the words are done, putting them on a page takes twenty minutes. Then read the whole page aloud as if you were the stranger: under thirty seconds, or something is earning its place badly. Cut it.

### The two-sentence privacy promise

Write this as a promise, not as legal language. Two sentences. The first says
what you will send and how often. The second says what you will never do.

> *"You'll get the [lead magnet name] straight away, then one short email a week
> with [what your Day-1 promise says]. I will never sell or share your email
> address, and you can unsubscribe at any time with one click."*

Two things to get right. First, the frequency must match what you actually plan to send — if your Day-1 promise says one email a week, say one email a week here, and then send one email a week. A subscriber promised weekly emails who gets daily ones feels misled, even if the emails are good.

Second, "never sell or share" must be true — true if you use a reputable email platform and don't hand the list to anyone. It stops being true the moment you share the list or buy addresses and mix them in. So don't.

If specific privacy rules apply where you operate, check what they require — this book is not legal advice. But don't hide behind legal language either.

### Free page options, compared honestly

You need a page on the internet with your headline, your bullets, your field and your button. There is no perfect free option — only the one whose trade-off you can live with.

**Option 1: Your email platform's hosted signup page.** Most email platforms
offer a simple hosted signup page or form as part of their service. This is the
option this book recommends for most beginners, because the page and the list
are the same system: someone signs up, they are on your list, and your welcome
sequence can start automatically. No connecting pieces together, no extra
account. The trade-off: the page will look plain. That is fine — plain and working beats beautiful and disconnected.
Before you commit, check the current free tier — free tiers change, and this book does not list limits that may be out of date. Make sure it covers a signup form, a list, and an automatic welcome email (Chapter 5).

**Option 2: A page on a free website or landing-page builder.** More control over how the page looks, and a web address that looks like yours. The trade-off is the connection: the form has to send subscribers to your email list, which means connecting two services — a Chapter 5 job, and one more thing that can break. If you don't already have a website you are happy with, don't build one to hold a single form.

**Option 3: A simple page linked from your social profiles.** A link-in-bio page holding your signup link is the fastest option. The trade-off is the extra click — your reader clicks twice, and every extra click loses people. Use it for distribution (Chapter 6), not as a replacement for a real signup page.

**Option 4: A page on a site you already own.** Make it a standalone page with no navigation — the same six parts, nothing else — not a form squeezed into a sidebar, which competes with everything else on the page and usually loses.

Don't collect addresses in direct messages, comments, or a spreadsheet. It doesn't scale, it gives subscribers no unsubscribe link, and it leaves you holding addresses with no record of what they agreed to. From day one, addresses go through a proper signup page, into a proper list.

Whichever option you choose, the test is the same: can a stranger reach the page from a link, understand it in seconds, and subscribe with one field and one button? If the tool makes that hard, the tool is wrong for this job.

### Mobile-first checks: read it like a stranger on a phone

Most of your first subscribers will meet this page on a phone — probably while doing something else, probably on a connection that isn't perfect. Check it on a real phone, not in a preview window on your laptop.

1. **The headline fits without scrolling sideways.** If it wraps to four lines
   or runs off the edge, shorten it.
2. **The headline, bullets and button are visible without scrolling — or the button after one short scroll at most.**
3. **The email field is big enough to tap, and tapping it opens the email keyboard** (the one with the @ key handy). A small field means mistypes, errors, and people leaving.
4. **The button is big enough to tap with a thumb, and it says what it does.** If you have to zoom in to tap it, it is too small.
5. **Nothing overlaps, nothing is cut off, nothing needs pinching to read.** Hand the phone to someone else and watch them use it without explaining anything — where they hesitate is where the page is unclear.
6. **The page loads quickly.** If it is slow, the cause is usually a large image — this page needs none.
7. **Test the whole journey.** Enter your own email address, press the button, and check what happens: do you get the lead magnet? Do you get a welcome email? Do this on your phone, with an address you actually check — if the handover is broken today, you will find out from a stranger's silence instead of from your own test.

Your reader will skim the headline, glance at the bullets, and decide. Write for the skim, not for the careful reader.

### What conversion rate to expect — and what to fix first when it's low

Sooner or later you will ask: *is my page working?* Measure it: subscribers divided by visitors. Your page tool will usually show both numbers; if it doesn't, count them yourself for a week. You need both: ten subscribers from twenty visitors is a very good page, and ten from a thousand is a page with a problem.

What counts as good? There is no single honest number: warm visitors — people
who already know you, or who arrived from a post that described the lead magnet
exactly — convert far better than cold strangers from a vague link. So distrust
any number you hear online, including any number attached to a tool's marketing;
your page's number only means something against your own page's number last
week, with the same kind of visitors.

Use the rate to diagnose. The rule is: **fix the traffic first, then the
promise, then the page.**

**First, check who is visiting.** If the people arriving don't match your one person, no headline will save the page. The fix is in where you share the link (Chapter 6), not on the page — and if you don't know who visited, you have a measurement problem, not a page problem.

**Second, check the promise.** If the right people arrive and leave, the promise is probably wrong or unclear — most often a mismatch: the post promised one thing and the page promises something slightly different. Make the headline match the promise that brought them there, word for word where you can.

**Third, check the page itself — in this order:** too many fields (remove the extras — the most common fix); a button that doesn't say what happens (name the lead magnet); a headline about you, not them (rewrite it with the reader as the subject); vague bullets (replace each with something done); a page slow or broken on a phone (rerun the mobile checks — a page that doesn't load converts nobody).

Change one thing at a time, and give it enough visitors to judge — changing everything at once tells you nothing about which change helped, and a handful of visitors tells you nothing at all. If your list is still very small, the most useful number isn't the conversion rate. It is the replies: did the people who subscribed answer your welcome email? Chapter 7 returns to why replies matter more than rates at a hundred subscribers.

A low rate on a page shown to the right people is information, not failure. Ask
a few of the people who didn't subscribe what nearly convinced them, and what
put them off — their answers are worth more than any advice about best
practice, including this chapter's.

### What not to do today

- **Don't build a website.** One page, six parts. A website is a different
  project for a different month.
- **Don't add a pop-up.** A pop-up before a stranger has read anything is an
  interruption, not an invitation.
- **Don't add testimonials you don't have.** A made-up or borrowed quote will be found out, and it costs you the trust the whole list is built on. (Chapter 8 shows how to use real ones.)
- **Don't promise a bonus you haven't made.** One lead magnet, honestly
  offered.
- **Don't hide the unsubscribe.** Every email must let people leave easily, with one click — people who can't leave mark you as spam. (Chapter 5 covers the settings.)
- **Don't share the link before you test it.** One test subscription from your
  own phone, all the way through to receiving the lead magnet, before the link
  goes anywhere public.

**Pre-publish checklist**

- [ ] The headline names my one person and the result, in their words.
- [ ] The subheadline says what the lead magnet is and how long it takes.
- [ ] Exactly three bullets, each naming something done, each fitting on one
      line.
- [ ] One field (email only) and one button, labelled with the lead magnet's
      name.
- [ ] The two-sentence privacy promise is under the button, its frequency
      matches my Day-1 promise, and every word of it is true.
- [ ] Nothing else is on the page: no menu, no life story, no pop-up, no testimonial or bonus I haven't made.
- [ ] Checked on a real phone: headline readable at a glance, button reachable
      after at most one short scroll, field opens the email keyboard, nothing
      overlaps or needs pinching.
- [ ] I subscribed with my own email on my phone and received the lead magnet.
- [ ] The page's promise matches the words I will use when I share the link.

Keep this template with your Day-2 production checklist. Together they are your
