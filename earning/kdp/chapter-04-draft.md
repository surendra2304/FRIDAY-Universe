# Your First 100 Subscribers — Chapter 4 (Draft)

## Chapter 4 — Day 4: Your Welcome Sequence, Email by Email

By the end of today you will have written five short emails — not a newsletter archive, not a funnel that runs for months. Five emails, in a fixed order, that turn a stranger who just handed you their address into someone who knows who you are, what you do, and why they should open the next one.

That is the whole job of a welcome sequence: the first conversation after the handshake. On Day 2 you built the lead magnet; on Day 3, the page where people trade their email for it. Today you write what happens after they click subscribe — when a new subscriber is paying the most attention they will ever pay you. If the first thing they get is silence, or a hard pitch, the moment passes. If they get the thing they asked for, plus a human being on the other end, they stay.

### What the five emails do, in order

Here is the arc. The order matters — do not skip ahead to the invitation before you have earned it.

1. **Deliver + story.** Give them the lead magnet immediately, and introduce yourself.
2. **Teach.** Solve one small piece of the problem, completely, in the email
   itself.
3. **Teach + proof.** Teach a second piece, and show it works — your result, a subscriber's, or a worked example.
4. **Invite.** Invite them to one next step — your offer, service, community, or paid thing — plainly stated.
5. **Ask a question.** Ask something real and invite a reply — the email that starts conversations.

Notice what is not in the list: no email that just says "welcome to my newsletter!" and nothing else, no email that is only a discount code, and no email that talks about you for five paragraphs before getting to the reader's problem. Every email earns its place by doing something for the reader. The shape is deliberate too: four emails that give, one that asks, and one that asks for something even simpler — a reply.

### The 80/20 rule: four parts value, one part offer

Pitch in every email and people learn to ignore you. Never mention what you sell and people assume you have nothing to sell — then act surprised when you finally do. The balance for a small list is simple: **about four-fifths of what you send should be useful on its own; about one-fifth can ask for something.**

In this sequence the ratio is built in: Emails 1, 2, 3 and 5 are value — the lead magnet, two lessons, a genuine question — and Email 4 is the offer. Count it in emails, not sentences. The test: if you removed every sentence asking the reader to buy, book, click or join something, would the email still be worth opening? For Emails 1, 2, 3 and 5, yes. For Email 4, no — that is its job.

The ratio describes the sequence as a whole, not every email — Email 3 mentions your offer in passing, and Email 5 asks for a reply. But do not let it become a reason to hide your offer: five generous emails that never mention you sell anything leave readers surprised when you finally do, while five pitches leave nobody opening the sixth. Generous first, asking clearly once. Both halves are required.

### Subject lines: patterns, not magic words

A good subject line for your first 100 subscribers is a clear one. These people signed up yesterday; they know who you are. The subject line's job is to tell them, honestly, what is inside — and to sound like a person, not a marketing department.

Six patterns cover almost everything this sequence needs. Steal the patterns, not the exact words.

1. **The delivery line.** "Here's your [lead magnet name]." Plain, and exactly what it says. Use it for Email 1.
2. **The specific lesson.** "The [one thing] that [specific result]." For example: "The one caption change that got a clinic its first booking from Instagram." The specificity is the trick — it names a real result for a real person. A vague version ("Marketing tips for your business") tells the reader nothing.
3. **The mistake line.** "The mistake I made with [the thing]." It promises a lesson the reader can use without making the mistake themselves — but only use it if the email genuinely describes a real mistake, yours or one you watched happen.
4. **The short question.** "Quick question about [their problem]?" Use it for Email 5.
5. **The plain invitation.** "Want help with [the problem]?" Use it for Email 4. It says what it is: people who want help will open it, and people who don't will skip it — which is correct.
6. **The continuation line.** "One more thing about [yesterday's topic]." For Emails 2 and 3 — a sequence is a conversation across days.

Three rules apply to all of them. **Write the subject line after you write the email** — one written first tends to promise something the email never delivers. **Never write a subject line you would be embarrassed to explain** — no fake urgency, no "Re:" when there was no earlier email. And **keep a list of the subject lines you use, and which ones get replies** — replies are the metric that matters at this size, as Chapter 7 explains.

### Before you write: three decisions

Make three decisions before you draft a word. They take five minutes and save
you from rewriting the sequence twice.

**First, the timing.** Email 1 sends immediately, the moment someone subscribes. Emails 2 to 5 send one a day after that, or every other day if you would rather stretch the sequence out. Either rhythm is fine — daily suits an urgent problem, every other day a slow-burning one. Pick one and do not change it halfway through — what matters is hearing from them while they still remember signing up.

**Second, the sender name.** Your own name, or your business name if people know it better — the same name that was on the signup page. Not "noreply", and do not change it between emails. The reader should see the name and know, without thinking, that this is the person who promised them help.

**Third, what Email 4 invites them to.** You cannot write the invitation until you know what it invites them to. It need not be a product — a free call, a paid session, a workshop, a small service, a community, or a paid version of the lead magnet all count. But it must be one specific thing, with one specific next step — not "check out my website". If you have nothing to offer yet, Email 4 can invite them to reply and tell you what help would look like — said plainly, and meant. Chapter 8 covers selling to a small list in full; for today, just name the one thing.

Each email below gives you the same four things: subject-line pattern, purpose, structure, and a worked skeleton — the bones of the email, with square brackets marking where your words go. The skeletons are not finished emails to copy and send. A skeleton filled with your real specifics will always outperform a polished email copied from someone else's business.

### Email 1 — Deliver + story

**Subject line:** The delivery line. "Here's your [lead magnet name]."

**Purpose:** Deliver exactly what was promised, immediately, and introduce yourself as a real person with a real reason for caring about this problem. This is the most-opened email you will ever send them: delivery and first impression, both at once.

**Structure:** Five short parts, in this order — short enough to read on a phone in under two minutes.

1. **One line of greeting, with the delivery attached.** The lead magnet link or file comes first — in the first two lines, before anything else. The reader signed up for the thing; give them the thing before you ask them to read about you.
2. **One line telling them what to do with it first.** The single first step, not a description of the whole document: "Start with page one, the checklist — it takes ten minutes." A clear first step gets used; no instruction gets saved for later, which means never.
3. **Your story in three to five sentences.** Why you care about this problem — the moment it became yours, not your life history or your credentials. If you are the physiotherapist who spent a year posting on Instagram and getting nothing but likes from other physiotherapists, say that. If you are the Etsy seller who listed twenty products nobody found, say that. The story proves you have stood where they are standing.
4. **What to expect next.** How often you will write, and what the next email is about: "I'll write once a week. Tomorrow I'll send you the one fix I'd make first if I were starting your [thing] today." This turns a one-off download into a list someone stays on.
5. **One small invitation to reply.** A door left open, not a demand: "If you get stuck on step one, hit reply and tell me where." Those replies are the beginning of everything Chapter 7 talks about.

**Worked skeleton — fill in the brackets:**

> Subject: Here's your [lead magnet name]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> Here's the [lead magnet name] you asked for: [link or attachment].
>
> Start with [the first step]. It takes about [realistic time].
>
> I'm [your name]. [One sentence about who you are, in plain words.] I care
> about [the problem] because [the moment it became your problem — two or three
> sentences, specific, in your own words].
>
> I'll write to you [how often]. Tomorrow I'll send you [the subject of Email 2
> in one line].
>
> If you get stuck on [the first step], just hit reply and tell me where — I
> read every reply.
>
> [Your name]

**What not to do in Email 1:** Do not pitch anything, and do not apologise for the email. Do not make the reader scroll past biography to reach the link. And do not promise a frequency you will not keep — if you say every Tuesday, it has to be every Tuesday. Trust is built from kept promises, and Email 1 makes the first one.

### Email 2 — Teach

**Subject line:** The specific lesson. "The [one thing] that [specific result]."

**Purpose:** Prove, with something useful, that subscribing was a good decision. Email 1 earned the open; Email 2 earns the second open, the third, and the habit of opening — by solving one small piece of the problem completely, inside the email itself. Not by pointing at a blog post. Not by teasing a course. In the email.

**Structure:** Four parts.

1. **One line connecting to yesterday.** "Yesterday you got the [lead magnet].
   Here's the first thing I'd do with it."
2. **The lesson, in steps.** One problem, one fix, in three to five numbered steps, each doable in minutes with something the reader already has. It must come from your Day-1 avatar work: the exact problem your one person described in their own words. For the physiotherapist, rewriting one clinic Instagram bio so it says who the clinic helps and how to book, instead of listing qualifications. For the Etsy seller, rewriting one product title with the words buyers actually search for. Both are small, specific, and finished in one sitting. Pick the fix you would give a friend in ten minutes — the fastest visible result — and save the slow lessons for your weekly emails.
3. **A worked example.** The before and the after, in the reader's world: "Before: [the vague version]. After: [the specific version]." Readers learn from examples far faster than from advice, and an example proves you have actually done this.
4. **One line pointing forward.** "Tomorrow I'll show you what happened when [someone] tried this." Just a reason to open the next email.

**Worked skeleton — fill in the brackets:**

> Subject: The [one thing] that [specific result]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> Yesterday you got the [lead magnet name]. Here's the first thing I'd do
> with it.
>
> [The problem in one sentence, in the reader's words — e.g. "You post three
> times a week and nobody books."]
>
> [The fix, in three to five steps — each starts with a verb and names something concrete to do today.]
>
> 1. [Step one — what to do, with what, in how long]
> 2. [Step two]
> 3. [Step three]
>
> Here's what it looks like in practice:
>
> Before: [the vague version your reader is probably using now]
> After: [the specific version, with the change explained in one line]
>
> Try it on [one thing — one post, one listing, one page] today. Tomorrow I'll show you what happened when [a real person, or you] tried this.
>
> [Your name]

**What not to do in Email 2:** Do not teach three things at once — an email that lists seven tips teaches nothing, because the reader does none of them. One lesson, fully done, beats a list of ideas. And do not end with a pitch dressed up as a lesson: if the lesson only works when the reader buys something, it is an advertisement, and the reader knows the difference.

### Email 3 — Teach + proof of concept

**Subject line:** The mistake line, or the continuation line. "The mistake I
made with [the thing]" — or "One more thing about [yesterday's topic]."

**Purpose:** Teach a second lesson, and show it works. Email 2 said "here is a useful thing"; Email 3 adds "and here is what happened when it was used". That second half — the proof — is what separates a teacher from a tip sheet. It need not be dramatic: a small, honest, specific result beats a grand claim every time, and a grand claim you cannot support costs you the trust Emails 1 and 2 built.

**Structure:** Four parts.

1. **One line connecting to Email 2.** "Yesterday I showed you [the fix]. Today, the thing that goes with it." One conversation, not five disconnected broadcasts.
2. **The second lesson, in steps.** Same shape as Email 2 — one problem, one fix, three to five steps, doable today — but building on Email 2 rather than repeating it. If Email 2 was rewriting the bio or the title, Email 3 might be what happens once someone actually finds you: the booking link, the first message, the follow-up.
3. **The proof, honestly told.** What happened when you, or someone you worked with, tried this: what was tried, over what period, and what actually changed — including what did not change. If the result was small, say it was small. "One clinic changed its bio and booking link on a Tuesday; by Friday it had its first booking from Instagram" is a real story. If you have no result of your own yet, use a worked example: a real page, listing or post — yours, or a public one with identifying details removed — walked through before and after. Never invent a customer, a result, or a testimonial. If you have no proof yet, say what you are testing now and what you will report back; honesty about an unfinished test proves you test things instead of making them up.
4. **One line pointing to tomorrow.** "Tomorrow I'll tell you how to get help with this directly, if you want it." That warns the reader an invitation is coming, so Email 4 does not feel like an ambush.

**Worked skeleton — fill in the brackets:**

> Subject: The mistake I made with [the thing]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> Yesterday I showed you [Email 2's fix in one line]. Today, the thing that
> goes with it — and the mistake I made before I learned it.
>
> [The mistake in two or three sentences — what you did, why it seemed reasonable, what it cost you.]
>
> Here's what I do instead now:
>
> 1. [Step one]
> 2. [Step two]
> 3. [Step three]
>
> Here's the proof it works — the honest version:
>
> [What was tried, over what period, and what actually changed. If the result was small, say so. If something did not work, say that too.]
>
> Tomorrow I'll tell you how to get help with [the problem] directly, if you
> want it.
>
> [Your name]

**A note on proof when you are just starting:** Most readers of this book will not have case studies yet — you are on Day 4 of building a list, not year four. Your proof can be your own before-and-after, a single real result, or a careful worked example. What it cannot be is a borrowed success story presented as yours, a screenshot you did not earn, or numbers rounded up until they are fiction. Chapter 8 returns to this rule. A small true story builds a list; a big invented one destroys it the first time a subscriber asks a follow-up you cannot answer.

### Email 4 — Invite

**Subject line:** The plain invitation. "Want help with [the problem]?"

**Purpose:** Invite the reader to the next step — the one specific thing you decided on before you started writing. This is the only email in the sequence whose main job is to ask for something. The reader has the magnet, has used two lessons, and has seen proof. Now tell them, plainly, how to go further.

**Structure:** Four parts. Keep it the shortest email after Email 1 — an invitation that rambles stops sounding like one.

1. **One line naming the problem again.** "If [the problem in their words] is still the thing in your way, here's the next step." Readers who recognise it keep reading; the rest can skip it without feeling sold to.
2. **The invitation, in plain words.** What it is, what happens, and what it costs — in time, money, or both. Name the price, if there is one, and the time commitment. If it is free, say so and say what happens on the call, so nobody has to guess whether it is a sales trap. If you have nothing to sell yet, the invitation can be a free call where you help with the problem and learn what people need — but say that exactly.
3. **Who it is for, and who it is not for.** One sentence each: "This is for [the one person from Day 1] who [the specific situation]. It is not for [the person it would not help]." This tells the right reader "this is for me" and the wrong reader not to waste their time.
4. **One clear next step.** One link, one button, one reply instruction — not three options. If they have to choose between four links, they will choose none.

**Worked skeleton — fill in the brackets:**

> Subject: Want help with [the problem]?
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> If [the problem in their words] is still the thing in your way, here's the
> next step.
>
> [What you offer, in one or two sentences: what it is, what happens, how
> long it takes.]
>
> It costs [price, or "nothing — it's a free [length] call"]. [What happens next after they say yes.]
>
> This is for [the one person] who [the specific situation]. It's not for
> [the person it would not help].
>
> If that sounds like you, [the one next step — reply with X / book here:
> link].
>
> [Your name]

**What not to do in Email 4:** Do not apologise for selling ("Sorry for the salesy email"). Do not use false urgency — no countdown that restarts, no "only two spots left" when there are not, no deadline that is not real. Do not send a second invitation the next day to people who did not respond: one invitation, clearly made, is respectful; a repeated one is pressure, and pressure turns a new subscriber into an unsubscribe. If nobody takes you up on it, that is information, not failure — Chapter 8 explains how to diagnose what went wrong. And if somebody does, reply fast and personally — the speed of your reply is part of the offer.

### Email 5 — Ask a question

**Subject line:** The short question. "Quick question about [their problem]?"

**Purpose:** Start a conversation. This is the most important email in the sequence for the long-term health of your list, and the easiest to underestimate, because it asks for nothing you can measure in money. It asks for a reply — and a reply tells you, in the reader's own words, what they are struggling with, what language they use, and what they need next. Those replies become your next lead magnet, lessons, and offer — and the reason Chapter 7 says replies matter more than open rates. A list that talks back is a list you can serve; one that never answers is a list you are guessing at.

**Structure:** Three parts. This is the shortest email of all — a long question email reads like a survey, and surveys get deleted.

1. **One line of context.** "You've had the [lead magnet] for a few days now,
   and I've sent you [the two lessons]." That is all the preamble it needs.
2. **The question itself.** One question, specific, answerable in a sentence or two. Not "What do you think?" — too big to answer. Not "What are your goals?" — that is a coaching session, not an email reply. Ask about the problem, in their world, right now: "What's the hardest part of [the thing] for you right now?" "What have you tried so far, and what happened?" "If you could fix one thing about [the problem] this week, what would it be?" Pick the question whose answer would most change what you write next.
3. **A promise about the reply.** "Hit reply and tell me — I read every reply, and I'll answer." Then keep it. If twenty people reply, answer twenty people: at this size you can, and a list of a hundred people who get personal replies is worth more than ten thousand who get an automated "thanks for your message".

**Worked skeleton — fill in the brackets:**

> Subject: Quick question about [their problem]?
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> You've had the [lead magnet name] for a few days now, and I've sent you
> [the two lessons in a few words].
>
> Can I ask you one thing?
>
> [Your one question — specific, answerable in a sentence or two. E.g.: "What's the hardest part of getting patients from Instagram right now — getting seen, or turning views into bookings?" Or: "What have you tried to get your Etsy listings found, and what happened?"]
>
> Just hit reply and tell me. I read every reply, and I'll answer.
>
> [Your name]

**Why this email works:** Replying is a small, human action — small enough to do from a phone, in a queue, in thirty seconds. Buying is a big decision; booking a call is a medium one. Every reply lowers the barrier to the next interaction, and teaches the reader's inbox that your emails are conversations, not broadcasts.

**What not to do in Email 5:** Do not attach a survey link instead of asking for a reply — a link to a form is a task; a reply is a conversation. Do not ask three questions; ask one, and ask it well. And do not send this email and then go silent for a month: the replies are the beginning of your weekly rhythm (Chapter 7). Answer them, note what people actually say, and let their words shape what you send next.

### Before you file the sequence away

In Chapter 5 you will set the sequence up so it sends itself. For today, your job is only the writing — but first, check the five skeletons against these common failures:

- **The delivery is buried.** Move the link into the first two lines of Email 1.
- **The story is longer than the help.** Cut the story.
- **The lesson needs a purchase to work.** It is not a lesson — rewrite it so it works on its own.
- **The proof is borrowed.** If the result is not yours, a real person's with permission, or a worked example you built, replace it with an honest statement of what you are testing.
- **The invitation has three next steps.** Reduce it to one.
- **The question needs an essay to answer.** Shorten it.

One last honest note: the sequence will not be perfect the first time. Email 4 may get no replies; Email 5 may get two, or twenty. That is normal, and fixable — once the sequence exists, you can change one email at a time. A sequence that exists and is slightly wrong beats a perfect one that lives in your head. Write it today; improve it next week, with real replies in front of you.

### Your finished asset: the five email skeletons

Collect the five skeletons in one place — your Day-4 asset, ready to type into your email platform in Chapter 5. Copy each skeleton, fill in the brackets, and read each aloud before saving it. If it sounds like you talking to one person about one problem, it is ready.

**Email 1 — Deliver + story.** "Here's your [lead magnet name]." Magnet first, first step, your story, what to expect, invite a reply.
**Email 2 — Teach.** "The [one thing] that [specific result]." One lesson in steps, before-and-after, point to tomorrow.
**Email 3 — Teach + proof.** "The mistake I made with [the thing]." Second lesson, honest proof, warn the invitation comes tomorrow.
**Email 4 — Invite.** "Want help with [the problem]?" Problem restated, offer with price and time, who it is/isn't for, one next step.
**Email 5 — Ask a question.** "Quick question about [their problem]?" One line of context, one question, promise you read every reply — and keep it.
