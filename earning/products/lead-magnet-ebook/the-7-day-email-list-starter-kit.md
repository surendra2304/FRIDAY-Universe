## Chapter 1 — Day 1: One Person, One Problem (full draft)

Maya had 4,000 followers and 11 email subscribers.

She had done everything the advice told her to do. She posted three times a week. She had
a link in her bio. She even had a freebie — a 27-page guide called *The Ultimate Wellness
Starter Pack*, which she had spent two weekends building. In three months, 11 people had
downloaded it. Nine of them never opened a single email after that.

Maya is a composite, but if you coach, create, or consult for a living, you know her. The
problem was not her effort, and it was not her expertise. The problem was the freebie
itself: it was made for everyone, so it was wanted by no one.

That is the trap this whole kit is built to avoid. Most lead magnets fail before a single
word is written, because they answer a question nobody is actually asking. *The Ultimate
Wellness Starter Pack* promises everything and therefore nothing. A tired new mum scrolling
at 11 p.m. does not want a starter pack. She wants to know what to eat tomorrow so she
stops crashing at 3 p.m. A freelance designer does not want a branding masterclass. He
wants a price list he can send without apologising for it.

So Day 1 has one rule, and it governs everything that follows: **one person, one problem,
one promise.**

### Who is the one person?

Not your audience. Not your niche. One person — specific enough that you could picture
them at their kitchen table. Answer these three questions, in writing, before you do
anything else:

1. **Who are they when they find you?** Not who you wish they were. The person as they
   are today: what they do, what they have already tried, what they are tired of.
2. **What are they stuck on right now?** Not their life's biggest problem. The small,
   sharp, annoying one that is on their mind this week.
3. **What would "fixed" look like by Friday?** A concrete, visible result. Not "feel
   more confident" — that is a mood. "Send the price list without rewriting it four
   times" is a result. "Know what to cook for the week in 20 minutes on Sunday" is a
   result. If you cannot picture it, the problem is still too big.

Write the answers down. They will feel too narrow. That feeling is the whole point. A
freebie that tries to help everyone gets downloaded by no one, because no one recognises
themselves in it. A freebie that describes one person's exact Tuesday gets shared in group
chats.

### Say the promise out loud

Once you know the person and the problem, write the promise as one sentence:

> **I help [person] [get this result] without [the painful part].**

Three examples, from three different kinds of creator:

- *I help busy new mums plan a week of dinners in 20 minutes, without a single
  "what's for dinner" panic at 5 p.m.*
- *I help freelance designers send a price list clients say yes to, without the
  three-day apology email beforehand.*
- *I help first-time managers run a one-to-one their team actually looks forward to,
  without a 40-slide deck nobody reads.*

Read yours out loud. If it sounds like a slogan, it is still too vague. If it sounds like
something your person would text a friend — "I found this thing that finally fixed my
Sunday meal panic" — it is right.

The promise is not marketing fluff. It is a filter. Every decision for the rest of the
week runs through it: if a chapter, a bullet, or a bonus does not serve this exact
promise, it gets cut. That is how a short eBook stays useful instead of becoming another
27-page starter pack.

### The format follows the promise

Beginners usually pick the format first ("I'll write an eBook!") and then hunt for a
topic to fill it. Do it the other way around. The promise tells you what the reader must
*do*, and the doing tells you the format:

- If the reader must **remember steps in the moment**, make a checklist.
- If the reader must **fill something in**, make a template.
- If the reader must **understand one idea before acting**, make a short guide — three
  pages, not thirty.

Maya's fix was a one-page checklist: *The 3 p.m. Crash Fix — 5 swaps for tomorrow's
lunchbox.* Same expertise, same audience, one-tenth the pages. It converted at eleven
times the rate of the starter pack, because it named the problem the reader already had.

### Today's 20-minute action

Set a timer. Write the three answers, write the promise sentence, and pick the format.
Then stop. Do not build the landing page today. Do not survey a hundred people. Do not
name the freebie yet — names come from results, and you have not promised one yet. If the
sentence is hard to write, that is useful information: the audience is still blurry, and
blurry is fixable in twenty minutes now, but expensive to fix after you have built
something.

Tomorrow, we turn the promise into the actual download — the smallest version that still
delivers the result. And once the download exists, the path into your paid work writes
itself: the freebie proves you can help with this one problem, and your offer is simply
the next problem, solved the same way.

One person. One problem. One promise. That is Day 1 — and it is the only day you cannot skip.
