## 3. Worked sample 1 — solo teaching episode (Basic tier, script only)

**Fictional show:** *The Freelance Ledger* — a weekly show for people in their first two
years of freelancing. **Fictional host:** Priya Nair. **Episode promise:** price a first
freelance project without guessing — one worksheet, three numbers, no hourly-rate trap.
Measured voiceover length is stated in the delivery notes at the end of the sample.

### Title options

1. **Price Your First Project Without Guessing (The Three-Number Worksheet)**
2. Stop Charging by the Hour: A Beginner's Pricing Worksheet

### SCRIPT

#### COLD OPEN (0:00–1:10)

Quick question. If a client asked you right now, "What would this project cost?" — could
you answer in one sentence, or would you do the thing most new freelancers do, which is
say a number, immediately regret it, and spend the whole project resenting it?

That regret is not a confidence problem. It's a missing worksheet. Today I'll give you
the worksheet: three numbers you write down before you quote anything, so the price you
say out loud is a price you can actually defend — to the client, and to yourself at
eleven o'clock at night when you're three hours into a project you priced at two.

And to be clear about what this episode is not: it's not a pep talk. There's no "know
your worth" speech coming. There is arithmetic. Boring, liberating arithmetic — the
kind that lets you say a price calmly, because you can show your working, even if the
only person you ever show it to is you.

*[THEME MUSIC — 5 seconds, fade under]*

#### INTRO (1:10–2:10)

Welcome to The Freelance Ledger. I'm Priya. This is the show for your first two years of
freelancing — the part nobody puts in the brochure.

Today: pricing your first project. Not "charge what you're worth" — that sentence has
never paid anyone's rent. Instead, three numbers: your floor, your time, and your risk.
By the end of the episode you'll have all three for a project you're quoting this week,
and one sentence you can say to a client without flinching.

Here's the route. First, your floor: the smallest amount a project can pay and still be
worth your week. Second, time and risk: how long the work honestly takes, and what
could make it take longer. Third, the sentence itself — how to say the number out loud
and then, crucially, stop talking. Grab a pen. This is a worksheet episode.

#### SEGMENT 1 (2:10–4:00) — Number one: your floor

Your floor is the smallest amount a project can pay and still be worth your week. You
get it from your own life, not from the market. Write down what one month actually
costs you — rent, food, software, the tax you'll owe. Not the aspirational month. The
real one.

Then divide by the days you can realistically bill. Not twenty-two. If you're honest,
it's closer to twelve to fifteen, once you count the emails, the admin, and the days
where the work just goes slowly. That daily figure is your floor rate. Below it, every
project quietly loses you money, no matter how good it looks in your portfolio.

A quick example, with round numbers — use your own. Say your real month costs 2,000,
in whatever currency you bill in. Say you can honestly bill fourteen days a month.
2,000 divided by fourteen is about 143 a day. That's your floor rate. A project that
takes five honest days has to pay at least 715 before it's even worth discussing —
and remember, that's the walk-away line, not the quote. The quote comes later, after
time and risk have had their say.

Here's the part people skip: the floor is not your price. It's your walk-away line.
You never quote it. You just refuse to go under it. And you recalculate it whenever
your life changes — new rent, new software bill, new tax bracket. A floor from two
years ago is a floor for a person who no longer exists.

#### SEGMENT 2 (4:00–6:20) — Numbers two and three: time and risk

Number two is time — but not the time you hope the project takes. The time it takes
when something goes wrong, because something always does. List every step: the kickoff
call, the first draft, the revisions, the file handover, the invoice you'll chase. Put
an honest duration next to each one. Then add a quarter again. That buffer is not
padding; it's the difference between a quote and a fantasy.

Notice what belongs on that list: the invisible work. The email thread where the
client changes their mind twice. The file format they forgot to mention. The "quick
call" that is never quick. New freelancers price the visible work and donate the
invisible work, and then wonder why a two-day project ate their week. It ate their
week because it was always a five-day project. They just quoted the poster, not the
film.

Number three is risk. Ask three questions. Is this client new, or have they paid you
before? Is the brief written down, or is it a voice note and a vibe? Is there a fixed
deadline, or a "whenever"? Each "risky" answer adds a percentage to your price — ten
percent each is a sane starting point. Risk you don't price is risk you donate.

And be specific about what risk actually looks like, because it rarely announces
itself. A new client is risk because you don't know how they pay. A vague brief is
risk because "make it pop" can mean four extra rounds. A floating deadline is risk
because the project will expand to fill your calendar. None of these make a client
bad. They just make the work bigger than the description — and bigger work costs more.
That's not punishment. That's arithmetic.

Now multiply: your floor rate, times your honest days, plus your risk percentage.
That's your project price. One number. No hourly rate in the email, no itemised menu
for the client to negotiate line by line.

#### SEGMENT 3 (6:20–7:40) — Saying the number out loud

Here's the sentence. "For the full project — [name the deliverables] — it's [price],
with half up front and half on delivery." Then stop talking. The silence after the
price is not awkward; it's the sound of you not negotiating against yourself.

If they push back, don't discount the price — shrink the scope. "We can drop the
second revision round and bring it to [lower price]." The work gets smaller; your rate
never does.

Two more sentences worth rehearsing. The deposit sentence: "I book the slot when the
first half lands." Not "sorry, I need a deposit" — just the policy, stated like
weather. And the silence sentence, which is no sentence at all: after the price, let
them speak first. Whoever talks first after a price is negotiating, and it should not
be you, against yourself, before they've even objected.

And if they go quiet entirely? That's information too. A client who disappears at a
fair price was never buying the work — they were buying the discount. The worksheet
didn't lose you that project. It saved you from it.

#### SPONSOR SLOT (7:40–8:00)

*[House promo — 30 seconds]* The Freelance Ledger worksheet pack — the floor-rate
calculator, the time-and-risk sheet, and the quote email from this episode — is linked
in the show notes. It's the same three numbers, on paper, ready to fill in.

#### LISTENER QUESTION (8:00–9:00)

This week's question is from Sam, who asks: "What if my floor rate makes me more
expensive than everyone else in my city?"

Then your floor is telling you something useful: either your costs are higher than your
market, or you're selling to the wrong clients. Both are fixable, but neither is fixed
by quoting below your floor and hoping volume saves you. Volume at a loss is just a
faster way to be tired. Find the clients whose problem is worth your floor — they're
usually the ones who've been burned by the cheap option already.

One more thing, Sam: "everyone else in my city" is doing a lot of work in that
question. You don't know their floor. You don't know if they're profitable, or busy,
or quietly looking for a job. Price from your own worksheet. The market is a rumour;
your rent is a fact.

#### OUTRO (9:00–9:30)

So: floor, time, risk. Three numbers, written down before you quote. Your homework
this week is one line long — work out your floor rate, and write it somewhere you'll
see it before your next quote.

Next week: the deposit conversation — how to ask for half up front without apologising
for it. The worksheet from today is in the show notes. I'm Priya — thanks for listening
to The Freelance Ledger.

*[THEME MUSIC — out]*

### SCRIPT

#### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)

*[Sound of a pottery wheel, fading under]*

Every mug in Marta Lindqvist's studio has a price on it, and every price has a story —
including the one range she stopped making entirely, even though it sold out every
single market day. Today she explains why the best-selling thing in her studio was the
worst thing for her business.

*[THEME MUSIC — 5 seconds, fade under]*

#### INTRO (0:35–1:30)

Welcome to Studio Visits, the show where we sit in a working maker's studio and talk
about the decisions behind the work. I'm Daniel Osei.

Today I'm with Marta Lindqvist, a studio potter who sells at weekend markets and
through two local shops. We're going to talk about how she decides what to make, how
she prices a mug when the clay, the glaze, the firing and her own two hands all have to
be paid for — and the best-selling range she deliberately killed.

Marta, welcome.

*[Guest: thanks, brief hello]*

#### INTERVIEW BLOCK (1:30–16:00)

**Q1 — The studio in one picture.** "For someone who's never been here: what does a
normal Tuesday look like in this studio?"
*Follow-up if short:* "And which part of that day do you protect most fiercely?"

**Q2 — The killed bestseller.** "Let's start with the mystery from the intro. You had a
range that sold out every market day — and you stopped making it. What was it, and what
did the numbers look like when you actually sat down with them?"
*Follow-up:* "Was there one market day where you knew?"

**Q3 — Pricing a mug, out loud.** "Walk me through pricing one mug. Not the philosophy
— the actual list. Clay, glaze, firing, time, the market stall fee, the breakages."
*Follow-up:* "Which of those do new potters most often forget to charge for?"

**Q4 — Markets versus shops.** "You sell at markets and through two shops. What does
each channel demand from you that the other doesn't?"
*Follow-up:* "If you had to drop one tomorrow, which goes — and why?"

**Q5 — The wholesale conversation.** "When a shop asks for a wholesale price, how do
you answer without just halving your market price and hoping?"
*Follow-up:* "What do you say when the number they want is below your cost?"

**Q6 — What you'd tell a beginner.** "Someone listening is about to price their first
batch of anything handmade. What's the one mistake you most want them to skip?"
*Follow-up:* "And what's the first boring system you'd tell them to build?"

*[Host transition, ~16:00]* "We'll take a short break, and when we come back, Marta
shows me the notebook where every price in this studio starts."

#### SPONSOR SLOT (16:00–16:30)

*[Sponsor read — 30 seconds, supplied by the sponsor; host reads verbatim.]*

#### SEGMENT — THE NOTEBOOK (16:30–21:00)

*[Host narration over studio sounds]*

The notebook is exactly what it sounds like: one page per product, every cost written
down in pencil, updated when the clay supplier changes a price. Marta's rule, in her
words: "If it isn't in the notebook, it isn't in the price — and if it isn't in the
price, I'm paying for it myself."

*[Guest expands on one page: the mug from Q3, cost by cost.]*

#### OUTRO (21:00–22:00)

Marta Lindqvist — thank you. The three rules from today, for your own notebook: price
from costs, not from hope; count your own hands as a cost; and let a bestseller die if
the page says it should.

Next week on Studio Visits: a letterpress printer who turned down the biggest order of
